## THE SCRIPT

**COLD OPEN (0:00 – ~0:45)**

D.J.: (setting down coffee, straight to camera) Twelve percent. That's what pricing a house wrong the first week can cost your seller. Not one percent. Not a rounding error. Twelve percent of the sale price, gone — because of a number somebody picked on day one. I'm D.J. Paris. If you've got a seller right now who wants to list high and "see what happens," this is the fifteen minutes that pays for itself. Because the most expensive mistake in this business isn't the house that won't sell. It's the one that sells — for way less than it should have. Let's talk about it.

---

**SEGMENT 1: THE PROBLEM (~0:45 – ~3:30)**

D.J.: Here's the study. Zillow Research looked at how long a home sits versus what it finally sells for. The homes that sold fast — priced right, gone quick — those closed about one percent under list. Basically full price. The homes that sat around two months? Five percent under. And the ones that sat the longest, almost eleven months on average? Twelve percent under list.

Read that gap again. One percent versus twelve percent. And the only thing that changed is time on the market. The house didn't get worse. The kitchen didn't age eleven months' worth in eleven months. The market just watched it sit — and started to wonder what's wrong with it.

And that's the part that stings. We tell sellers "let's price high, we can always come down." We say it like coming down is free. It's not free. Every week that house sits, it's spoiling. Think of a listing like a carton of milk. It's got a sell-by date, and in this market that date is about ten days. Fresh, everybody wants it. Past it? Buyers scroll by and go, "why's that one still here?"

The seller thinks the risk of pricing high is "we might have to lower it later." No. The real risk is you sell — at twelve percent under — and nobody in that house ever knew they left forty, fifty grand on the table. It's the most invisible loss in real estate. Nobody sees the money that never showed up.

And it gets worse, because overpricing doesn't just cost time. It hides the listing from the exact people who'd have paid the most for it. That part's mechanical, not opinion, and I'll show you in a minute. But first, feel the size of this. Twelve percent. On a four-hundred-thousand-dollar house, that's forty-eight thousand dollars. That's not a haircut. That's the down payment on their next place — gone, because of a number somebody typed in on a Tuesday.

**SEGMENT 3: THE SYSTEM / SOLUTION (~6:30 – ~11:00)**

D.J.: The system's called the Freshness Window. Three moves, and you can run all three before your next listing appointment.

Move one. **Price the bracket, not the number.** Buyers don't shop in exact dollars. They shop in ranges. Four hundred to four-fifty. Four-fifty to five hundred. They set a search, they draw a line. So if the house is worth four-fifty and you list at four-fifty-five "just to have room" — you just fell off the top of the four-to-four-fifty search. The buyers who'd fight over it never see it. And you landed at the very bottom of the four-fifty-to-five-hundred search, where your house looks like the weakest thing on the page next to the four-ninety-nines. You want to be the best house in a bracket, not the worst house in the next one up. Five grand "for room" can cost you your entire best-buyer pool.

Quick one for the live crowd — tell me in the comments what price bracket most of your listings land in. I'm curious where everybody's working. Drop the range.

Move two. **Win the first ten days.** A brand-new listing gets a surge. Everybody with a saved search, everybody hitting the "new" filter, all of it lands in the first week to ten days. Think about how you shop for anything online. You sort by newest. Your buyers do the exact same thing to houses. Fresh gets clicked, old gets scrolled past. That surge is the only free shot at competition you will ever get on that house. That's where bidding wars are born. Waste those ten days on a test price and there's no button to get them back. The attention is spent.

And that seller you're holding in your head — the one who wants to go high? Almost time. Ninety seconds. Stay with me.

Move three. **Price to provoke.** Day-one pricing has exactly one job: trigger showings and offers. Not "leave room." Provoke a reaction. Price at or a hair under true market value and you don't lose money — you create the traffic that makes buyers compete, and competition is the only thing that has ever pushed a price above asking. Picture two identical units, same building, same floor plan. One lists right at market. Showings all weekend, buyers bumping into each other, sells over asking. The one across the hall lists higher to "test it." Quiet. Then a cut. Then another. Closes for less than the first one got. Same exact square footage. The only difference was the number on day one. That's the whole ballgame.

Now — I'll push on my own system, because you're already thinking it. "D.J., if I price at market and it sells in three days, I left money on the table. I priced too low." Fair. That's the fear that keeps every overpriced listing overpriced. Here's the honest answer. The house that sells in three days with four offers doesn't sell at market. It sells over, because you manufactured competition. The house that starts high and sits eleven months sells twelve percent under. One of those left money on the table, and it's not the fast one. Speed isn't the enemy of price. Speed is how you get the price.

---

**SEGMENT 4: THE HARD OBJECTION (~11:00 – ~12:45)**

D.J.: Alright, here's the objection. The real one, the one you're yelling at your screen right now. "D.J., that's great, but if I walk in with the honest number, the seller just signs with the other agent who promised them the high one. I don't lose twelve percent, I lose the whole listing." Yeah. That's the fear. And it's legitimate.

So here's what you do instead of caving. You don't argue the number, you show the milk. You put the Zillow gap on the table: one percent versus twelve percent. And you say, "the agent who quotes you the high number isn't getting you more money. They're getting your listing. Then in six weeks they'll ask you to cut it — twice." Then you hand them a plan. We list at the number that sells, and we agree right now, in writing, on a price-improvement date if there's no offer in the first ten days. No fight later. It's already decided.

And if they still pick the fantasy number and walk? You didn't lose a listing. You dodged one that was going to sit, expire, and eat your time while your name sat on a stale sign. Being the only honest person at that table is how you win the next three.

---

**SEGMENT 5: THE MATH / MOTIVATION (~12:45 – ~14:15)**

D.J.: Let's put real numbers on it. Take a four-hundred-thousand-dollar listing. Twelve percent is forty-eight thousand dollars. That's the seller's loss when a house goes stale. But run it through your side of the desk too. Your commission on that house, say three percent — the difference between selling at four hundred and selling at three-fifty-two is real money out of your pocket. On every overpriced listing. All year.

Now flip it. You take ten listings a year. If overpricing quietly shaves even a piece of that twelve percent off a few of them, that's tens of thousands of dollars you're handing back, split between your sellers and you, for the privilege of avoiding one uncomfortable conversation on day one.

The uncomfortable conversation is free. The comfortable number costs forty-eight grand. That's the trade. Every single time.

---

**CLOSE (~14:15 – ~15:00)**

D.J.: Recap, one line: the number you pick on day one is worth more than everything you do after it.

That seller you've been holding — here's exactly what to do. Open your phone and send this: "Hey [Name], been thinking about your price. Homes that sell the first week get near full price. Homes that sit get up to twelve percent less. Don't want that to be your house. Fifteen minutes before we list?" Send it before anything else today.

Two more: pull the three most recent solds in the bracket before your next appointment, solds not actives. And write a price-improvement date into every agreement, no offer by day ten and the number moves.

You've told yourself winning listings means agreeing with the seller's number. The agents who protect their sellers' money say the number nobody wants to hear on day one, not day sixty. You just sent that text. That's not a mindset. That's a thing you did thirty seconds ago.

Fresh sells. Stale sits. Nobody pays for week-six milk. See you next week.
