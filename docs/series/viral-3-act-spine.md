# The Viral 3-Act Spine

This is the structural lens that sits over every series in this repo. It does not replace the
universal [`editorial-standards.md`](../editorial-standards.md) or any series standard. It regroups
the rules you already follow into three acts a writer can *feel* while drafting, and it fixes the
one thing that most separates a video people finish from a video people scroll past:

**The middle of a short-form video should be a story, not a briefing.**

**If this document and the universal standard ever conflict, the universal standard wins.** In
particular, nothing here overrides Rule 1 (never fabricate) or Rule 7 (length discipline). Story is
a tool, not a license to add length or invent scenes.

---

## Why this exists

Retention dies in the middle. The hook earns the first three seconds; the close lands the action.
The 40 seconds in between are where viewers leave -- and they leave when the middle reads as
*exposition* ("here's the background, here's my take") instead of *story* ("here's what happened,
and then here's the turn").

The repo already had every part of a viral structure: the open-loop hook (Rule 2), the mirror
moment (Rule 3), the loop-back close (Rule 9.1). What it didn't have was a single mental model that
forces the middle to behave like a story. That is what the three acts do.

The six beats in Rule 3 (Hook, Mirror, Agitation, Fix, Reframe, Close) still exist. They are the
*texture* inside the three acts. The acts are how you think; the beats are what you write.

---

## The three acts

### Act 1 -- HOOK (0:00-0:03): stop the scroll AND promise the payoff

Everything in universal Rule 2 still applies (the five allowed hook types, the captions.ai
spoken-line constraint, the 3-second test). The 3-act spine adds one demand on top:

**The hook must name the reward the viewer gets for staying.** A scroll-stopper that doesn't
promise a payoff opens no loop. The viewer stops, but has no reason to finish.

- **Stops the scroll, no promise (weak):** "The CEO of Realtor.com just called out private listings."
- **Stops the scroll AND promises the payoff (strong):** "The CEO of Realtor.com just called out
  private listings. And there's one word in his argument that gives away his real motive."

The second version makes the viewer need to reach the payoff. That is the loop the whole video then
closes.

### Act 2 -- STORY (the middle ~60%): earn the payoff through tension, not exposition

This is the act the spine exists to fix. The middle is where you deliver context, but the discipline
is *how* you deliver it.

**Write the middle as a story with a turn, not a list of facts.** A story has:

- **A subject.** A specific person, room, moment, or unfolding event the viewer follows. Not "the
  industry" -- a named CEO who drew a line. Not "most agents" -- the agent on a Tuesday staring at
  fourteen untouched leads.
- **A turn.** The "but then..." / "now here's the turn" / "here's where it gets worse" moment. The
  story changes direction at least once. A straight line of facts is a briefing; a turn makes it a
  story.
- **Micro-loops.** Every line should pull to the next one. End beats on a small open question the
  next sentence answers, then reopens. This is the 15-second re-hook (Rule 2) applied continuously,
  not just once.

**The test:** read the middle aloud. If you could reorder the sentences without breaking anything,
it's a list, not a story. A story has an order that can't be shuffled, because each beat sets up the
next.

The four story modes by series type are in the table below. Pick the one your series uses.

The modes below answer *what kind of tension* the middle runs on. A related but separate question is
*whose story it is and what arc it takes* -- the ten first-person story shapes in
[`../storytelling-formats.md`](../storytelling-formats.md), rated for fit against D.J.'s brand.
Formats 2 (lead with proof, then teach) and 10 (tell someone else's story) both change what Act 2
looks like without changing the three acts themselves.

### Act 3 -- PAYOFF (the last ~25%): resolve the loop, hand over the takeaway, loop back

The close is not just a CTA. It's where the loop the hook opened gets *paid off*. Three things,
stacked, in this order:

1. **Resolution.** Close the loop. Deliver the thing the hook promised. The "aha." If the hook
   promised "the word that gives away his motive," the payoff has to name it.
2. **The takeaway.** The exact words, exact action, or exact frame the viewer keeps. This is the
   existing "Here's what you do now" close (Rule 4). Still an action at the viewer's own life, never
   an engagement ask.
3. **The loop-back.** The final line recontextualizes the hook so it hits harder on rewatch (Rule
   9.1). Same discipline as before; now it's explicitly the last of the three payoff moves.

A payoff that delivers the action but never resolves the hook's promise leaves the loop open and the
viewer unsatisfied. A payoff that resolves the loop but hands over no action is a story with no use.
You need all three.

## Story is mandatory for some series, compressed for others

Story takes time. Density is the other superpower of this content, and Rule 7 (cut every second past
45, 30-45s is the sweet spot) is non-negotiable. For tactical content, a full three-act narrative
adds length the format doesn't want. So the spine applies at two intensities:

| Series | Type | Story intensity |
|---|---|---|
| **Inside the Industry** (NF / IS / IA) | Narrative | **Full 3-act mandatory.** The material has a story; tell it. |
| **Podcast Promo** (Hype Machine) | Narrative | **Full 3-act mandatory.** The episode is the story; the promo is the trailer. |
| **The Playbook** | Tactical | **Compressed.** The scenario IS the one-line story. Don't expand it. |
| **What Actually Works** | Research-backed | **Compressed.** The "why the obvious fix fails" turn IS the story beat. One turn, then the mechanism. |
| **tapthis Walk-and-Talk** | Product-led | **Compressed.** The mid-stride frustration IS the story beat. Stay tight. |

**Compressed** means: keep the hook-promises-payoff discipline (Act 1) and the resolve-takeaway-
loopback discipline (Act 3), but Act 2 is a *single story beat* -- one scene, one turn -- not a
developed narrative. The scenario, the failed-fix turn, or the mid-stride frustration is already a
micro-story. Land it in one or two sentences and move to the payload. Do not bolt a three-act arc
onto a 30-second tip. If story would push a tactical script past its length cap, the length cap wins.

**The fabrication guardrail still binds harder than story.** "Make the middle a story" is never a
reason to invent a scene, a character, a "last Tuesday she..." detail, or a quote. Rule 1 and Rule
8.2 (specificity must be earned) override the story instinct every time. The IS-002 and PB-001
failures both came from reaching for a detail that made the line better but wasn't true. A real
briefing beats a fabricated story.

## The four story modes (Act 2 by series type)

The middle is always a story, but the *kind* of story depends on what the series has access to.

### Conflict unfolds -- News (NF)
The story is the event itself, told as a conflict with a turn. Who did what, the twist, why it
implicates the viewer. "He drew a line. Then here's the turn -- he benefits from drawing it. And
he's still right." The viewer follows the conflict, not a summary of it.

### Discovery unfolds -- Synthesis (IS)
The story is D.J. noticing a pattern across the archive. "I kept hearing the same thing from people
who shouldn't have anything in common." The named guests are the characters; the pattern is the
reveal. The turn is the moment the pattern clicks. Never quantify the pattern with an invented
number (Rule 1).

### Scene unfolds -- Access (IA)
The story is the room D.J. was in. "I was sitting across from NAR's executive team when someone
said the quiet part out loud." First-person, specific, a scene the viewer couldn't have been in. The
turn is what surprised D.J. in the room.

### Mirror-moment scene -- Tactical (Playbook / WAW / tapthis)
The story is the viewer's own moment, dropped into mid-stride. "It's Tuesday. You've got fourteen
leads you haven't touched in three weeks." The viewer is the character. **Compressed:** this is one
or two sentences, then straight to the fix. The mirror moment is the story; don't develop it past
the point of recognition.

## The Story Pass (added to the build workflow)

The standard walk-and-talk build is draft -> stress test -> EP polish (see
[`../automation/walk-and-talk-on-demand.md`](../automation/walk-and-talk-on-demand.md)). The stress
test now runs the Story Pass before anything else:

1. **Hook promises a payoff?** Does the first line name the reward, not just stop the scroll? If it
   opens no loop, rewrite it.
2. **Middle is a story, not a briefing?** Is there a subject and a turn? Could you reorder the
   sentences without breaking it (a list) or not (a story)? If it's a list, find the turn and build
   around it.
3. **Each line micro-loops?** Does every sentence pull to the next, or do some just sit there
   delivering a fact? Cut or rewrite the sitters.
4. **Payoff resolves the hook's promise?** Does the ending deliver the specific thing the hook
   promised, then the action, then the loop-back? If the loop the hook opened is still open at the
   end, the payoff is incomplete.
5. **Length still holds?** For tactical series, did the story discipline push it past the cap? If so,
   compress Act 2 back to one beat. Length wins.

If the script is a narrative series and fails check 2, it goes back to draft. If it's a tactical
series and passes the old checklist but reads as flat exposition, apply the compressed story beat --
don't expand it into a full arc.

## Quick reference

```
ACT 1  HOOK     stop the scroll AND promise the payoff (open the loop)
ACT 2  STORY    subject + turn + micro-loops (NOT exposition; NOT invented)
ACT 3  PAYOFF   resolve the loop -> hand over the action -> loop back

Narrative series (NF/IS/IA, Podcast Promo): full 3-act, mandatory.
Tactical series (Playbook, WAW, tapthis): compressed -- one story beat, length wins.
Rule 1 and Rule 7 override the story instinct. Always.
```
