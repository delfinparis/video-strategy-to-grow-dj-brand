# What Actually Works — Problem Index (Content Engine)

This is the runway for the **What Actually Works** series: a ranked bank of real problems
real estate agents have, each mapped to a non-obvious, research-backed mechanism that
actually fixes it. It is the source feed the series draws from, the same way
`scripts/news_brief.py` feeds the NF (news) slots.

This is **not** the series standard (format rules, length, metadata) — that's a separate
doc. This is the idea bank: what to make, in what order, and why each one works.

> **Status:** Concept / pre-launch. **Add, don't replace** — Inside the Industry stays primary
> (the April 18 data shows news is the measured engagement winner; this is unproven). Pilot at
> ~1/week now with its own clean attribution baseline; the July 19 pivot review is the decision
> point on whether to ramp. Stress-tested and guardrailed 2026-06-20. Built 2026-06-20.

---

## The series in one line

> Inside the Industry tells agents **what's happening**. What Actually Works tells them
> **why they're stuck and the specific, research-backed move that fixes it** — in a way a
> non-realtor would still want to watch and share.

The realtor framing is always load-bearing (it's the recruiting credibility and what keeps
the advice specific instead of generic). The universal mechanism is what makes a non-realtor
share it. Broad reach is a *side effect* of being specifically useful — not the goal.

## Non-negotiable guardrails (read before scripting anything)

This series swims in the most crowded niche on social media — "research-backed
communication / persuasion / self-improvement." The only thing that keeps D.J. from being a
worse, less-credentialed version of Jefferson Fisher or Andrew Huberman is **the moat**:
700 interviews, a brokerage-operator's seat, NAR access. These five rules exist so the engine
can't drift generic. **If a concept can't clear all five, it doesn't get made.**

**G1 — The archive (or operator) tie-in is REQUIRED, not optional.**
Every video must carry the proof layer: a *real, verified* top producer from the 700-episode
archive who already does this intuitively ("I've watched hundreds of top agents do exactly
this — they just never had a name for it"), or a firsthand brokerage-operator observation.
This is what re-bridges D.J.'s actual authority to borrowed science. No verified tie-in → no
script. Never fabricate or composite a guest (universal Rule 1).

**G2 — The science is the explanation, never the headline.**
Position every video as *"the science behind what top producers already do"* — not "here's a
self-help tip." The 700-episode credential stays in the driver's seat; the research explains
*why* it works. This keeps D.J. in the lane he owns instead of one he doesn't.

**G3 — The "strip test." Apply to every concept before it's greenlit:**
> *"Could this exact video run on a generic self-help account with the real estate parts
> removed?"*
If yes, kill it or sharpen the agent application until the answer is no. This single question
protects the whole series from collapsing into mush.

**G4 — Beachhead first: two categories only at launch.**
Lead with the two that are both highest-pain-for-agents *and* most moat-aligned:
**(a) sales communication / persuasion** (700 live examples) and **(b) prospecting /
follow-up discipline** (the agent's #1 self-sabotage). Park the pure life/mindset entries
(sleep, burnout, finances) — that's where D.J. is most replaceable. Widen the aperture only
after the beachhead proves out.

> **Beachhead roster (launch-eligible entries):** #1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 16, 17,
> 18, 19, 26. Everything else (productivity, mindset, life, money, client-experience) is
> **parked** until the beachhead proves out.
>
> **First slate = Tier 1 ∩ beachhead → #1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8.** (Tier 1's #6 peak-end and #7
> mental contrasting are strong but off-beachhead; they wait.)

**G5 — Source rigor + the do-not-cite list.**
Every claim verified to a primary source before scripting. No "studies show." And never cite
a replication-crisis casualty, even though they're famous and tempting:

| Do NOT cite | Why |
|---|---|
| **Power posing** (Cuddy) | Failed to replicate; the original author walked back the effect. |
| **Ego depletion** (willpower as a finite fuel tank) | Large multi-lab replications failed. |
| **Generic "priming"** (money/elderly-words priming behavior) | Core social-priming results did not hold. |
| **Strong-form Mozart effect** | The lasting-IQ claim was overstated; effect is tiny/transient. |
| **Learning styles** (visual/auditory/kinesthetic) | No evidence tailoring to "style" improves outcomes. |

When in doubt, prefer findings with a meta-analysis or multi-lab replication behind them
(implementation intentions, "but you are free," affect labeling, anchoring, loss aversion).
Choice overload (entry #10) is *contested* — only use it with the replication caveat noted.

**Beyond the five:** vary the hook. The "what you think is right is actually wrong → real
mechanism" reveal leans on the contrarian / "it's not X, it's Y" patterns flagged in
`ai-tells-field-guide.md`. Rotate openings so the format doesn't read formulaic *or*
AI-generated across a run of videos.

## The format every entry must serve (five beats)

1. **The problem** — a specific friction the viewer actually feels (ideally the
   *second-order* problem: not "you should prospect," but "you know you should and you don't").
2. **Why the obvious fix fails** — the hook that earns the next 25 seconds.
3. **The mechanism** — one named, citable research finding. The non-obvious *why*.
4. **The application** — one thing, do it tomorrow, framed for an agent.
5. **The close** — watchpoint or the soft recruiting CTA.

If an entry can't fill all five beats, it's not ready to script.

---

## How to use this index

- **Clear all five guardrails first** (G1–G5 above). They override tier and score. An entry
  with no verified archive tie-in (G1) or one that fails the strip test (G3) does not get made,
  no matter how high it scores.
- **Launch from the first slate (#1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8).** After that, pull from the beachhead
  roster. Park everything off-roster until the beachhead proves out.
- **One mechanism per video.** Never stack two findings; the second-order problem is enough payload.
- **Verify the source before scripting** and check it against the do-not-cite list (G5).
  Re-confirm every finding to a primary source (study, book, or meta-analysis). No "studies
  show." Name the researcher and the work.
- **Find the archive tie-in, don't fabricate it.** The `Archive hook` note flags where to look,
  but the guest must be **real and verified** before naming them (universal Rule 1; no composite
  or invented attributions). No tie-in found → the entry waits until one is.
- **Mark entries as used** in the registry section at the bottom once aired, so the same
  mechanism doesn't repeat.

### Scoring rubric (1–5 each; used to set the tier)

- **Pain** — how acute is this problem for a working agent?
- **Surprise** — how non-obvious is the mechanism? (Generic = low. "I didn't know that" = high.)
- **Reach** — how shareable is it to a non-realtor audience?
- **Recruit** — how strongly does it signal "I'd want this person as my broker"?
- **Citable** — how solid/defensible is the research?

## Tier 1 — Launch slate (make these first)

| # | Problem (agent-felt) | Why the obvious fix fails | The mechanism (source) | The agent application | P/S/R/Rc/C | Archive hook |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "I know I should follow up with leads, and I still don't." | "Be more disciplined" has no trigger, so it never fires. | **Implementation intentions** — "*when* X, I will Y" plans lift follow-through ~2–3x (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006 meta-analysis). | Don't write "follow up." Write "when I pour my 8am coffee, I dial the top 5 in my CRM." The cue does the work. | 5/4/5/5/5 | Top producer with a fixed daily call ritual |
| 2 | "I feel like I'm bothering people when I reach out." | You assume your check-in is an imposition; it isn't. | **Undersociality** — people badly underestimate how much others appreciate being reached out to (Epley; Liu et al., 2023; Sandstrom). | Text three past clients today before you talk yourself out of it. You're the only one calling it annoying. | 5/5/5/4/4 | Guest who built a business on "just checking in" |
| 3 | "My client got emotional and I made it worse by explaining." | Trying to *fix* the feeling escalates it. | **Affect labeling** — naming an emotion measurably lowers its intensity (Lieberman et al., 2007, UCLA fMRI). | Seller angry at a lowball? Don't defend it. Say "you're frustrated this came in this low." Temperature drops, then you negotiate. | 5/5/5/4/4 | Negotiation-heavy top producer |
| 4 | "The harder I push to close, the more they pull back." | Pressure triggers resistance, not agreement. | **Psychological reactance** (Brehm, 1966) + **"But you are free"** — affirming the person's freedom to say no roughly doubles compliance (Carpenter, 2013 meta-analysis of 42 studies). | End the ask with "it's completely your call." Giving the out is what gets the yes. | 4/5/5/4/5 | Guest known for low-pressure selling |
| 5 | "I talk too much in listing presentations." | You think pitching harder wins; it doesn't. | **Question-asking likability** — people who ask more follow-up questions are better liked and trusted (Huang, Yeomans, et al., 2017). | Cut your pitch in half. Replace it with three follow-up questions about *their* situation. They'll like you more and tell you how to win. | 4/4/5/4/4 | Top lister who "interviews the client" |
| 6 | "Clients don't remember me after the deal closes." | You optimize the average experience; memory doesn't work that way. | **Peak-end rule** (Kahneman, Redelmeier) — people remember the peak moment and the ending, not the average. | Engineer one peak and a deliberate ending (closing-day gift, handwritten note at the door). That's what they'll repeat to friends. | 4/4/4/5/4 | Guest with a signature closing ritual |
| 7 | "I visualize success but nothing changes." | Positive fantasy alone *drains* motivation. | **Mental contrasting / WOOP** (Oettingen) — pairing the wish with the real obstacle outperforms pure visualization. | Don't just picture the closed deal. Name the obstacle ("I'll skip my calls when I'm tired") and the if-then for it. | 4/5/5/3/5 | — |
| 8 | "Cold-call reluctance — I freeze before dialing." | You overestimate how much the other person judges you. | **Spotlight effect** (Gilovich et al., 2000) — we massively overestimate how much others notice and judge us. | The prospect isn't scrutinizing your opener. They forgot the call by lunch. Dial the next one. | 5/4/4/4/4 | Guest who beat call reluctance |

## Tier 2 — Strong bank (the deep middle)

| # | Problem | Why the obvious fix fails | Mechanism (source) | Agent application | Category |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9 | "I don't ask for referrals — feels pushy." | You assume people will say no; they say yes far more than you predict. | **Underestimating compliance** (Flynn & Lake/Bohns) — we systematically underrate how often a direct ask gets a yes. | Make the direct ask this week. The "yes" rate will surprise you. | Prospecting |
| 10 | "Buyers can't decide and stall forever." | More options feels generous; it paralyzes. | **Choice overload** (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000 — note later replication debate). | Curate to three homes, not thirty. Fewer choices, faster decisions. | Persuasion |
| 11 | "I lose listings to agents with bigger numbers." | Leading with credentials triggers comparison, not connection. | **Unity principle** (Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion*, 2016) — people say yes to "one of us." | Lead with the shared *we* — same neighborhood, same school, same situation — before the stats. | Persuasion |
| 12 | "My pricing conversations go sideways." | Whoever names the first number frames the whole talk. | **Anchoring** (Tversky & Kahneman). | Set the anchor deliberately with comps before the seller blurts their dream price. | Persuasion |
| 13 | "I can't focus — I'm juggling ten things." | Multitasking leaves a cognitive drag you can't feel. | **Attention residue** (Leroy, 2009) — switching tasks leaves part of your mind on the last one. | Batch like work. One call block, one email block. Stop checking between. | Productivity |
| 14 | "My mind races at night with unfinished tasks." | Willpower-ing yourself to relax doesn't release the loop. | **Zeigarnik effect** + the fix (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011) — writing the *next physical action* frees the mental grip almost like finishing. | End-of-day "open loops" dump: the literal next action for each loose thread. Brain goes quiet. | Productivity |
| 15 | "I keep dodging the boring admin (CRM, data entry)." | Discipline runs out; the chore stays boring. | **Temptation bundling** (Milkman et al., 2014). | Only watch your show / drink the fancy coffee *while* doing CRM entry. You'll start looking forward to it. | Productivity |
| 16 | "I give up on leads after one or two touches." | You think they need a *reason* to like you; familiarity alone does it. | **Mere-exposure effect** (Zajonc, 1968) — repeated low-key contact builds trust without new content. | Light, regular touches (a useful link, a quick note) compound into preference. Stay in the feed. | Prospecting |
| 17 | "I struggle to build rapport fast with new clients." | Small talk stays shallow; you never get to trust. | **Disclosure reciprocity / escalating questions** (Aron's "fast friends"). | Ask one slightly-more-personal question early. Reciprocal openness accelerates trust. | Communication |
| 18 | "Buyers hesitate even when it's the right house." | "Look what you'd gain" underperforms. | **Loss aversion** (Kahneman & Tversky) — losses loom larger than equivalent gains. | Frame the real risk of losing *this* home, honestly. Loss moves people more than gain. | Persuasion |
| 19 | "My closing gifts/thank-yous don't land." | Generic swag reads as marketing, not gratitude. | **Reciprocity, done right** (Cialdini) — unexpected, personalized, meaningful beats branded and bulk. | One specific, personal gesture > a hundred logo mugs. Make it about them. | Communication |
| 20 | "I psych myself out before big moments." | "Calm down" fights your body's arousal and loses. | **Reappraisal: anxiety → excitement** (Brooks, 2014) — saying "I'm excited" beats trying to calm down. | Before the presentation, say out loud "I'm excited." Same arousal, better performance. | Mindset |

| 21 | "I beat myself up after a bad call." | **Self-distancing / third-person self-talk** (Kross et al., 2014). | Coach yourself by name — "okay [name], next call" — to cut the anxiety and reset faster. | Mindset |
