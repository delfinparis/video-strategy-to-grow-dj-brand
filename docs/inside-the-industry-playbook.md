# Inside the Industry: Series Playbook

This document is the operating manual for the **Inside the Industry** series (Pillar 4). It exists to keep the series sharp and on-strategy as the script library grows.

**Before writing any new script in this series, read [`editorial-standards.md`](editorial-standards.md).** The editorial standards document defines the non-negotiable rules for every piece of content in this repo: no fabricated statistics, no engagement asks, no em dashes, and a pre-commit checklist every new script must pass. Those rules take precedence over anything in this playbook.

---

## Why This Series Exists

D.J. Paris's brand goals (in priority order):

1. Kale Realty recruiting in Chicago
2. NAR relationship deepening
3. Keeping It Real Podcast subscriber growth
4. National thought leader reach

The other three pillars (AI Agent Minute, Agent Tip of the Day, The Playbook) serve goals 1, 3, and 4 reasonably well as utility content. None of them deliver goal 2 (NAR relationship) or build the kind of authority that makes recruiting meaningful.

Inside the Industry exists to do the job that utility content cannot: position D.J. as someone with **direct insider access** to the people and decisions shaping the industry, and as someone with **the willingness to have a sharp opinion** about what he sees.

The series is modeled after three reference creators:

- **Steven Bartlett (Diary of a CEO)** - borrows authority from access to high-status guests rather than self-declaring expertise
- **Ryan Serhant** - combines industry takes, personal humanity, and education in a single content matrix
- **"School of Hard Knocks" interview clips** - proves that signaling access (not having a huge following) is the engine of authority content

## The Three Sub-Types

Every Inside the Industry script is one of three types. The type is set in frontmatter (`type:`) and determines the script structure.

### Type A: Access ("I just talked to...")

**File prefix:** `IA-`
**Hook pattern:** "I [met / spent time with / was in the room with / talked to] [specific person or group]. Here's what I [noticed / learned / observed]."

**What it does:** Signals D.J.'s direct relationships with NAR leadership, industry titans, podcast guests, and decision-makers. The content is not a quoted interview. It's an observation that only someone in the room could make.

**What you must include:**

- A specific named person or group D.J. actually interacted with
- A specific observation, not a quote
- An interpretation or framing that shows editorial judgment
- A reframe that gives the audience something to take away

**What you must NOT do:**

- Put words in someone's mouth
- Claim a relationship that doesn't exist
- Repeat what's already public

**Example:** IA-001 ("I spent a day in a room with NAR's executive team")

**Production cadence:** As access events happen. Cannot be batch-produced. Each one is a real moment in D.J.'s calendar.

---

### Type B: Synthesis ("After 700 interviews...")

**File prefix:** `IS-`
**Hook pattern:** "I have interviewed [N+] real estate agents. Here's [the pattern / the one thing / what I noticed] that..."

**What it does:** Synthesizes patterns across hundreds of podcast guests into a sharp, opinion-forward observation. The 700-episode archive is the credential. No one else can credibly make these observations because no one else has done the work.

**What you must include:**

- A specific number of interviews referenced (700 is the anchor)
- A counter-intuitive or surprising observation
- A pattern that holds across multiple guests
- A teachable application

**What you must NOT do:**

- Make up patterns that aren't actually in the archive
- Cite a guest without checking they actually said it
- Soft-pedal an opinion to avoid controversy (sharp > safe)

**Example:** IS-001 ("After 700 interviews, here's what AI still can't fix")

**Production cadence:** Slower than other sub-types. Each script requires actual reflection on patterns. Aim for 1 per week maximum.

---

### Type C: Reactive ("News flash + my take")

**File prefix:** `NF-`
**Hook pattern:** "[Major industry news just dropped]. Here's what it [means / changes / signals]."

**What it does:** Reacts to breaking industry news (NAR settlements, Fed decisions, court rulings, major brokerage news, policy changes) within 24-48 hours, with a local Chicago angle and a clear point of view. Not neutral reporting. Not aggregation. Opinionated analysis.

**What you must include:**

- The news event with date and source
- A Chicago angle (even if the news is national)
- D.J.'s explicit point of view, not just facts
- A forward-looking implication ("here's what to watch for next")
- A close that follows one of the three acceptable patterns in `editorial-standards.md` Rule 2 (stop on reframe, action ask at the viewer's own life, or observation that implies the catalog) -- never an audience-ask close

**What you must NOT do:**

- React without verifying facts (Inside the Industry credibility depends on accuracy)
- Just summarize what HousingWire already wrote
- Hedge the take
- End with "follow me," "subscribe for more," or any audience-ask variant (banned by `editorial-standards.md` Rule 2)

**Example:** NF-001 ("NAR Settles Tuccori Lawsuit for $52M")

**Production cadence:** Reactive only. Triggered by news events. Aim for 2-4 per month, more during major news cycles.

## The X% Club Template

A reusable hook structure pioneered by D.J.'s "The 9% Club" post (4/1/2026), which drove 651 impressions on Personal LinkedIn - 62% of that week's total reach from a single post.

**The structure:**

1. **Counter-intuitive statistical hook** - "The X% Club: Why (100-X)% of agents are [missing outcome]"
2. **Validation that the thing works** - "the data has been consistent for years"
3. **The adoption gap** - "fewer than 1 in N actually do it"
4. **Reframe away from awareness** - "not a lack of awareness, but practical barriers"
5. **Named specific barriers** - list 3 concrete reasons
6. **Low-friction CTA** - "one [small action], [small time investment]"

**Why it works on Personal LinkedIn:**

- Counter-intuitive numbers stop the scroll
- Exclusivity framing ("the X% Club") makes it feel like joining a group
- Specificity beats generality ("3 named barriers" > "many obstacles")
- The CTA lowers friction so action feels possible

**Where to use it:**

The X% Club template can power Type B (Synthesis) scripts especially. Example: IS-002 ("The 6% Club: Why 94% of Top Producers Still Answer Their Own Phone").

The template is also reusable for The Playbook (Pillar 3) and even for AI Agent Minute (Pillar 1) where appropriate.

**The brief for any X% Club post:**

> Pick a behavior or habit that top performers do consistently and average performers don't. Find the actual ratio (or estimate it credibly). Frame it as a club. Name the barriers. End with one specific small action.

## Platform Strategy

Inside the Industry content is **LinkedIn-first**, **Personal Facebook second**.

This is data-driven (April 2026 Metricool baseline):

- **Personal LinkedIn**: 7,001 followers. 49% Greater Chicago. 31% Real Estate industry. 34% Senior. Top companies in audience: every major Chicago brokerage by name. NF-001 hit 5.7% engagement rate (3x LI average).
- **Personal Facebook**: 2,878 followers. 74.2% Chicago. Engagement up 122% in last 28 days.

These two platforms have the highest concentration of D.J.'s actual target audience for Kale recruiting.

**Distribution sequence for any Inside the Industry script:**

1. **Personal LinkedIn** (primary) - full long-form post text
2. **Personal Facebook** (secondary) - adapted from LinkedIn, slightly more conversational
3. **Personal Instagram (Reel)** - 22-30 second video version with caption
4. **TikTok** - 22-30 second video version with stronger hook
5. **YouTube Shorts** - 22-30 second video, search-optimized title

The script files include all five platform versions (D.J. does not use X/Twitter). LinkedIn is the canonical version because that's where the audience converts.

## The "Could Someone Else Say This" Test

Before any Inside the Industry script gets written, it has to pass one test:

> **Could someone else with a smaller following say this exact thing?**

If the answer is yes, the script is too generic. Make it more specific to D.J.'s access. Reference a specific person. Cite a specific number of interviews. Name a specific Chicago neighborhood or brokerage.

If the answer is no, the script is in the right lane.

This test is non-negotiable. Inside the Industry is not "general real estate commentary." It's "things only someone with D.J.'s specific access can say." The test enforces that.

## What Inside the Industry Is NOT

To stay sharp, the series has to refuse certain content types even when they're tempting:

- **Not a news aggregator.** If the news doesn't have a Chicago angle or a sharp take, don't react.
- **Not a tip series.** Tips belong in The Playbook (Pillar 3) or AI Agent Minute (Pillar 1).
- **Not a podcast promotion engine.** Inside the Industry should mention the podcast naturally, but it's not a series of podcast ads.
- **Not safe.** If the take could be made by any cautious commentator, it doesn't belong here.
- **Not generic.** If it could apply equally to a real estate agent in Phoenix, it's missing the local layer.

## No Follower Asks (Convention)

**Never end a script with "Follow me for more" or any variant.** No "subscribe for more." No "follow along if you want..." No audience-begging CTAs of any kind.

This is a convention, not a preference. It applies to the spoken script, the LinkedIn caption, the Instagram caption, the Facebook caption, the TikTok caption, and the YouTube Shorts description. Every surface.

**Why:** Follower asks signal "I'm still at the stage where I need you to follow me." The entire point of Inside the Industry is to signal the opposite -- "You should already be following me because of who I am and what I have access to." Asking for the follow undercuts the authority position the series is designed to build.

Reference models Steven Bartlett (Diary of a CEO), Ryan Serhant, and Jefferson Fisher all almost never end with "follow for more." Their content earns the follow because it's sharp, not because they begged.

**What to end with instead:**

Three acceptable closing patterns for any Inside the Industry script:

1. **Stop on the reframe.** Let the strongest line of the script be the last thing spoken or written. No CTA. Silence is a stronger close than a beg. Example (IS-001): "AI gives you use. Experience gives you judgment. One of those takes twenty years." Then cut.

2. **Action ask (not audience ask).** Tell the viewer to do something in their own life, not to engage with you. Example (IS-002): "Tomorrow morning, pick up one call you'd normally skip. See what happens."

3. **Observation that implies the catalog.** Hint that there's more without asking for the follow. Example (IS-003): "That's one pattern from 700 interviews. There's a whole catalog." The phrase "whole catalog" earns curiosity without begging.

**What NOT to write (banned list):**

- "Follow me for more..."
- "Subscribe for more..."
- "Follow along if you want..."
- "Hit the follow button..."
- "Don't forget to subscribe..."
- "Make sure you're following..."
- Any phrasing that makes audience growth the explicit ask

**Podcast mentions are different.** You can say "I cover this weekly on the Keeping It Real Podcast" or "More from inside the industry on the Keeping It Real Podcast." Those are informational, not audience-begging. They tell people where else to find D.J. without asking them to do anything.

**The test:** If the line would feel awkward coming from Bennett Richardson or Ryan Serhant, it's too needy. Cut it.

## Adding New Scripts to the Series

When writing a new Inside the Industry script:

1. Determine the sub-type (A, B, or C) and use the matching prefix (IA-, IS-, NF-)
2. Number sequentially within the sub-type (IA-004 follows IA-003, etc.)
3. Set frontmatter `series: "Inside the Industry"`, `type: "access" | "synthesis" | "reactive"`
4. Set `primary_platform` to "LinkedIn" by default unless there's a specific reason otherwise
5. Pass the "Could someone else say this" test before writing
6. Anchor every claim to a specific person, number, or observation D.J. can defend
7. Write the LinkedIn version first (canonical), then adapt down to the other platforms
