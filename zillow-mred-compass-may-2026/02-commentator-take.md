# Commentator Take Strategy

A memo on the strongest public position to own on this story, and why.

## The take, in one sentence

This is not Zillow versus Compass. This is the moment the MLS publicly stopped pretending to be neutral, and the agent class is too busy picking sides to notice they are the asset being fought over.

## Why this take wins

Every other commentator will land on one of three positions: pro-Zillow ("transparency for consumers"), pro-Compass ("sellers should choose"), or hand-wringing centrist ("both sides have a point"). All three are boring, and all three accept the same wrong frame: that the story is a fight between two companies over rules.

The compelling case sits one level above that. The real story is the second defendant in the lawsuit caption. MRED. The MLS. The supposedly neutral cooperative that just got caught using its rule-making power to enforce one brokerage's marketing strategy across the country.

That is the story nobody else is going to tell with conviction, and that is why it lands.

## How to argue it

### Open with the foundational myth

Organized real estate runs on one core fiction: that the MLS is a member-owned utility serving all agents equally. Brokers love this fiction because it gives them a moat without having to admit they have one. Agents love it because it lets them feel like independent operators on a level field. NAR loves it because the cooperative framing is what has kept federal regulators mostly off their back for fifty years.

The fiction survives only as long as the MLS does not pick winners.

### Point at the smoking gun

On May 6, MRED demanded that Zillow display the private inventory of one specific brokerage outside MRED's own territory, or lose access to listings inside it. MLS Grid, a feed company chaired by MRED's own CEO Rebecca Jensen, issued a parallel termination threat the same day. Compass holds three of MRED's roughly thirteen board seats, about 23 percent. Compass also controls roughly 35 percent of sales in the Chicago market. And Compass agreed to subsidize MRED membership for the first 100,000 Compass agents nationally, a step Zillow's complaint argues could triple MRED's size.

This is not an inference. It is in the complaint. The conflict of interest is not hiding in a smoke-filled room. It is in a glass conference room with the org chart on the wall.

### Refuse to make Zillow the hero

This is critical for credibility. Zillow's interest in transparency is not principled. It is a business model. Zillow needs public listings because public listings are how it sells Premier Agent ads to the agents who created them. But in this specific fight, the company whose financial incentives accidentally line up with consumer transparency is Zillow, and the company whose incentives line up with hiding inventory from buyers is the MLS. That is an industry that has lost the plot.

### Give Compass credit for being honest

Compass has never claimed to be neutral. Compass built a private-exclusive product, marketed it openly, and bet that sellers will pay for asymmetric access. Whether you like that bet or not, Compass is a brokerage acting like a brokerage. The party that is lying is the MLS, which still publishes the cooperative-utility narrative on its website while functioning as enforcement for one broker's marketing strategy.

### Turn it on the audience

The line that makes agents sit up: you are not the customer in this fight, and you are not the product. You are the supply chain.

Compass wants you in the building because the private network is worthless without thousands of agents to populate it. Zillow wants you on the platform because Premier Agent ad inventory is worthless without you bidding on it. The MLS wants your dues because the cooperative narrative is worthless without dues-paying members to point to. In every scenario, your listings and your leads are the inventory being monetized by someone else.

Pick a tribe. Pick consciously. Walled garden, portal volume, or your own brand. The one losing move is the most popular move: complain, wait, and assume the MLS will still be there in five years to keep things fair.

### Close with a prediction

This case settles within twelve months. MRED walks back the territorial threat. Compass keeps its private network. Zillow keeps its standards. Everyone declares victory in their press release.

But the precedent is set. Other MLSs are now watching: rule-making power can be used to extract concessions from portals on behalf of the dominant broker. The move will be tried again. The antitrust theory is on the shelf, ready to be pulled down.

Within five years, the MLS as a neutral cooperative is over. What replaces it is some mix of portal-direct relationships, brokerage walled gardens, and a handful of MLSs that survive by becoming explicit franchises of their dominant brokerage.

## Why this take is durable

- **Defensible if Zillow wins.** You predicted the MLS gets exposed.
- **Defensible if Zillow loses.** You predicted the MLS doubles into its captured role and accelerates its own irrelevance.
- **Defensible if they settle.** That is literally what you predicted.
- **Positions you as the structural-thinker** while everyone else is scoring the press-release fight.
- **Gives the audience a clear and uncomfortable action item.** That is the difference between commentary that gets nodded at and commentary that gets quoted and shared.

## What to avoid

- **Avoid bashing Compass agents.** D.J. runs a competing brokerage. Any print quote that reads as anti-Compass will be filed as competitive trash talk regardless of its merit.
- **Avoid predicting the legal outcome.** Predicting who wins the case is a coin flip and the wrong frame anyway. Predict the structural consequence either way.
- **Avoid the "good guy / bad guy" framing.** That is the line D.J. drew in his email to TRD. Hold the line.
- **Avoid recycling NAR-bashing.** The story is the MLS specifically, not the trade association generally.

## When to deploy which version

- **The full structural argument** belongs on the podcast, in long-form LinkedIn, in a blog post. Audience has 5+ minutes.
- **The reel and short captions** drop the meta argument and lead with one observable fact (the second defendant in the caption is the whole story) and one action (the three-call playbook).
- **The press interview** lives in between. Stay neutral on who should win. Stay sharp on what every agent should do this week.
