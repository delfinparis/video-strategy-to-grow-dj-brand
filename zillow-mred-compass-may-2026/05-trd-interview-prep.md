## Drafted answers

### Q1: What should agents know about this lawsuit? How does it affect their business operations at this point?

Operationally, nothing has changed yet. Listings are flowing through MRED, Zillow still has its Chicago feed, and no Chicago agent's pipeline shrank this week. This is going to be a long federal case. It will probably settle.

What has changed is the conversation in the seller's head. Every active seller in Chicago is reading these headlines today and wondering if their listing is at risk. The operational impact this week isn't on the MLS. It's on the agent's phone.

The single most important thing to do this week is call every active seller and say, in three sentences, that there's a lawsuit, here's what it does and doesn't affect today, and here's where the listing is appearing right now. The agents who make that call first look like experts. The ones who don't look like they don't read the news.

> **Quote:** "The operational impact this week isn't on the MLS. It's on your phone. The agents who call their sellers first win the next three months."

### Q2: Should agents at Compass-affiliated brokerages be thinking about this any differently? Are they at any disadvantage with respect to Zillow or off-market listings compared to other agents?

Yes, but not the way most people frame it. Compass agents aren't at a disadvantage. They have a real tool other agents don't. The private network and the three-phase strategy work, and the data behind them is legitimate. They also carry a real exposure other agents don't. The largest share of listings on Zillow's permanent ban list under Listing Access Standards has been Compass listings.

What I'd tell a Compass agent today is don't treat the private-exclusive playbook as automatic. Every listing is its own decision. Some sellers benefit from a walled-garden launch. Some sellers need full Zillow exposure on day one. The job is to match the strategy to the seller, not run one playbook on every house.

For agents at every other brokerage, the temptation is to feel like this is somebody else's problem. It isn't. The fight over private listings is a fight over who controls distribution for the next decade. Every agent has a stake.

> **Quote:** "Compass agents have a tool other agents don't and a risk other agents don't. Every listing is its own decision. There's no one playbook for every house."

### Q3: What are the potential longer-term ramifications for agents if Zillow succeeds and requires MRED/Compass to share their listing feeds?

If Zillow wins, the practical effect for an agent is more transparency, less separation from premium-brokerage tools, and more lead competition on the major portals. The walled-garden model gets weaker. Realtor.com, Homes.com, and Redfin get more confident setting their own standards.

If Zillow loses, MLSs nationwide will have learned that rule-making power is a tool they can apply against portals on behalf of their largest brokers. The walled-garden trend accelerates. Brokerages build more proprietary networks. Distribution fragments. Agents who aren't at one of those brokerages have fewer places where their listings get full reach.

The practical move is the same in either outcome. Don't build a business that depends on one portal sending you leads, or one brokerage's tools defining your value. The agents who win over the next two years already own their database, their past clients, and the way they talk to them. The ones who get hurt are on a single-channel pipeline.

> **Quote:** "The agents who get hurt over the next two years aren't on either side of this lawsuit. They're the ones with a single-channel pipeline."

### Q4: What does this signal about industry competition over access and transparency of listing data? Is it likely to set a precedent about the role of the MLS in sharing listing data, or the role of brokerages in cooperating with MLSs going forward? What would be the likely outcomes depending on if Zillow succeeds or fails?

This is the case that's going to decide what an MLS actually is. For fifty years we've called the MLS a neutral cooperative and let it write the rules. Nobody pushed the contradiction. This lawsuit pushes it.

If the court says an MLS can use its data-licensing power to require portals to display a specific brokerage's products, every MLS in the country has a new tool, and brokerages will lobby their local MLS to use it. If the court says it can't, the rule-making power of MLSs gets significantly constrained, and the next round of innovation in distribution moves to portals and brokerage walled gardens.

Either way, the next decade of real estate looks like one open public market or a set of competing private networks. The agent's job is to be ready for either version, which means owning the relationships nobody else can take away.

> **Quote:** "For fifty years we've called the MLS a neutral cooperative and let it write the rules. The court's about to make us pick."

## Fact-check reference for every claim in the answers

- **"There's a lawsuit."** Federal complaint filed 2026-05-12 in N.D. Illinois. Confirmed across all trade press.
- **"The largest share of listings hitting Zillow's permanent ban list under Listing Access Standards has been Compass listings."** Industry reporting (Open Homes Photography analysis, RISMedia coverage of LAS enforcement) indicates roughly 48 permanently banned listings under LAS as of recent enforcement, with roughly 90 percent attributed to Compass. If Caleb asks for the number directly, attribute to "industry reporting on LAS enforcement to date" and let his team do the source verification.
- **"More than half of consumer real estate search traffic goes through Zillow."** General industry knowledge; D.J. should not cite a specific percentage in the press answer without a sourced report in front of him. The drafted answer keeps it qualitative: "the single largest consumer real estate destination on the internet."
- **"A federal case will probably settle."** D.J.'s prediction, framed as opinion. Defensible because it is framed as opinion.
- **"For fifty years the industry has treated the MLS as both a neutral cooperative and a rule-making body."** General industry framing; the cooperative model dates roughly to the 1908 NAR founding and the modern MLS structure to the mid-twentieth century. "Fifty years" is rhetorically defensible shorthand; if Caleb pushes, switch to "for as long as anyone in the business can remember."
