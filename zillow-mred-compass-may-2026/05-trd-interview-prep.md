## Drafted answers

### Q1: What should agents know about this lawsuit? How does it affect their business operations at this point?

Operationally, nothing has changed yet. Listings are still flowing through MRED, Zillow still has its Chicago feed, and no Chicago agent's pipeline got smaller this week. This is going to be a long federal case. It will probably settle.

What has changed is the conversation in the seller's head. Every active seller in Chicago is reading these headlines today and wondering if their listing is at risk. That's the operational impact right now, the phone calls agents are about to get. The agents who get ahead of those calls look like experts. The ones who get caught flat-footed look like they don't read the news.

The single most important thing an agent can do this week is call every active seller and explain, in three sentences, that there's a lawsuit, here's what it does and doesn't affect today, and here's where the listing is currently appearing. That's the operational impact today.

> **Quote:** "The operational impact this week isn't on the MLS. It's on your phone. Every seller in Chicago is about to ask their agent about this, and the agents who have an answer ready win the next three months."

### Q2: Should agents at Compass-affiliated brokerages be thinking about this any differently? Are they at any disadvantage with respect to Zillow or off-market listings compared to other agents?

Yes, but not the way most people frame it. Compass agents aren't at a disadvantage. They have a real tool other agents don't, the private network and the three-phase marketing strategy, and the data behind it is legitimate. They also carry a real exposure other agents don't, which is that the largest share of listings hitting Zillow's permanent ban list under Listing Access Standards has been Compass listings.

What I'd tell a Compass agent today is the worst move is to treat the private-exclusive playbook as automatic. Every listing is its own decision. Some sellers benefit from a walled-garden launch. Some sellers need full Zillow exposure on day one. The agent's job, regardless of brokerage, is to match the strategy to the seller, not run the house playbook on every house.

For agents at other brokerages, the temptation is to feel like this is somebody else's problem. It isn't. The fight over private listings is a fight over who controls distribution for the next decade. Every agent has a stake.

> **Quote:** "Compass agents aren't at a disadvantage. They have a tool other agents don't, and they carry a risk other agents don't. The smart ones are making that call listing by listing instead of running one playbook on every house."

### Q3: What are the potential longer-term ramifications for agents if Zillow succeeds and requires MRED/Compass to share their listing feeds?

If Zillow wins, the practical effect for an agent is more transparency, less differentiation from premium-brokerage tools, and probably more lead competition on the major portals. The walled-garden model gets weaker. Other portals, Realtor.com and Homes.com and Redfin, will get more confident setting their own standards.

If Zillow loses, MLSs nationwide will have learned that rule-making power is a tool they can apply against portals on behalf of their largest brokers. That accelerates the walled-garden trend. Brokerages will build more proprietary networks. Distribution fragments. The agent who isn't at one of those brokerages has fewer places where their listings get full reach.

For the agent reading this, the practical move is the same in either outcome. Don't build a business that depends on one portal sending you leads, or one brokerage's tools defining your value. The agents who get hurt over the next two years are the ones with a single-channel pipeline. The agents who win already own their database, their past clients, and the way they communicate with them.

> **Quote:** "Win or lose, the lesson for an agent is the same. Don't build a business that depends on one portal or one brokerage to send you opportunities. The agents who get hurt over the next two years are the ones with a single-channel pipeline."

### Q4: What does this signal about industry competition over access and transparency of listing data? Is it likely to set a precedent about the role of the MLS in sharing listing data, or the role of brokerages in cooperating with MLSs going forward? What would be the likely outcomes depending on if Zillow succeeds or fails?

This is the case that's going to decide what an MLS actually is. For fifty years the industry has treated the MLS as a neutral cooperative and as a rule-making body at the same time, and it's worked because nobody pushed the contradiction. This lawsuit pushes the contradiction.

If the court says an MLS can use its data-licensing power to require portals to display a specific brokerage's products, every MLS in the country has a new tool, and brokerages will lobby their local MLS to use it. If the court says it can't, the rule-making power of MLSs gets significantly constrained, and the next round of innovation in distribution moves to portals and brokerage walled gardens.

Either way the precedent matters. This is one of the moments that decides whether the next decade of real estate looks like one open public market or a set of competing private networks. The agent's job is to be ready for either version, which mostly means owning the relationships nobody else can take away from you.

> **Quote:** "This is the case that's going to decide what an MLS actually is. The industry has been treating the MLS as a neutral cooperative and a rule-making body at the same time for fifty years. The court's about to make us pick."

## Fact-check reference for every claim in the answers

- **"There's a lawsuit."** Federal complaint filed 2026-05-12 in N.D. Illinois. Confirmed across all trade press.
- **"The largest share of listings hitting Zillow's permanent ban list under Listing Access Standards has been Compass listings."** Industry reporting (Open Homes Photography analysis, RISMedia coverage of LAS enforcement) indicates roughly 48 permanently banned listings under LAS as of recent enforcement, with roughly 90 percent attributed to Compass. The spoken answer uses "largest share" rather than the specific 90 percent figure because the exact count moves over time and the qualitative framing is more defensible in a press quote. If Caleb asks for the number directly, attribute to "industry reporting on LAS enforcement to date" and let his team do the source verification.
- **"More than half of consumer real estate search traffic goes through Zillow."** General industry knowledge; D.J. should not cite a specific percentage in the press answer without a sourced report in front of him. The drafted answer keeps it qualitative: "the single largest consumer real estate destination on the internet."
- **"A federal case will probably settle."** D.J.'s prediction, framed as opinion. Defensible because it is framed as opinion.
- **"For fifty years the industry has treated the MLS as both a neutral cooperative and a rule-making body."** General industry framing; the cooperative model dates roughly to the 1908 NAR founding and the modern MLS structure to the mid-twentieth century. "Fifty years" is rhetorically defensible shorthand; if Caleb pushes, switch to "for as long as anyone in the business can remember."
