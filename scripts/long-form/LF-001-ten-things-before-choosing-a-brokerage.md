# 10 things to know before you choose a brokerage

> **WOW: Criterion 6 (a checkable counterexample) stacked ten times. The whole recruiting conversation a new licensee is about to walk into is built on one number, and every person on the other side of the table benefits from that number staying the only one discussed. This hands them the other nine, in the order to ask them, from a guy who owns a brokerage and says so in the first thirty seconds.**

> **Strongest Line:** "You have no baseline right now. That's not a knock on you, you got your license on Tuesday."

---

## Spoken Script (~10:00, 1,548 words)

### COLD OPEN (0:00 - 1:11)

You passed. Congratulations, seriously.

Now every brokerage in your market wants to sponsor your license. And every single one of them is going to open the conversation with a split.

The split is the least important number in that conversation.

I've interviewed over 700 agents on the Keeping It Real Podcast, and I should tell you something up front. I run a brokerage in Chicago. So discount me accordingly.

There's a link below to grab fifteen minutes with me, and I'll tell you exactly why it's there. Some of you are going to want to ask me about mine. Most of you won't, and that's completely fine. You're getting all ten questions right here either way, and they work no matter who you point them at.

I've watched a lot of new agents pick wrong, and it's almost never because somebody handed them a bad split. It's because nobody told them what to ask.

So here are ten things, in the order I'd ask them.

Number five is the one that costs you the most, and almost nobody warns you about it. Number nine you won't feel for six years, and by then it's gone.

---

### 1. The brand is not going to get you your first deal (1:11 - 2:22)

Most new agents pick the name they've heard of, because when you don't know anything yet, a name you recognize feels like safety.

Here's the problem with that. Your first ten clients are going to be people who already know you. Your cousin. Somebody from your old job. A friend from the gym. Not one of those people is hiring a logo. They're hiring you, and they'd hire you if you worked out of a van.

Right now in Chicago there are about fourteen hundred licensed brokerage companies. Fourteen hundred. You can probably name twelve.

Greg Pekarsky co-founded his own firm here and built one of the top independent teams in the state. Matt Laricy runs his family's firm and has been at the top of this city for volume for years. Neither one of them has a national brand behind him.

Now, I'm not telling you a brand is worthless. A big brand can buy you a real training system, a referral network, and a sign people recognize on your first listing appointment.

I'm telling you it's a purchase. It has a price, and you should know the price before you decide it's worth it.

---

### 2. Get every fee in writing before you ever discuss the split (2:22 - 3:16)

The split is one line on a bill that has a lot of lines.

So before you talk about splits at all, send one email and ask for the complete list. Every recurring charge and every per-transaction charge. Desk fee, technology fee, errors and omissions, franchise fee, transaction fee, marketing fee, mandatory training fee. Whatever they've got.

Ask for it in an email, and ask them to reply in that email.

And I want you to pay attention to what happens next, because this is the real test. If somebody won't put their own fee schedule in writing, you've just learned the most useful thing you're going to learn all week, and it took you four minutes.

And when the lists come back and you have no idea whether any of it is normal, that's the thing I'm actually useful for. Link below, fifteen minutes, bring me the lists.

---

### 3. Run the math on your worst year, not your best (3:16 - 4:07)

Every plan you're going to be shown gets demonstrated at twenty transactions.

You're not doing twenty transactions. Almost nobody's first year is twenty transactions.

The Consumer Federation of America looked at this a couple of years ago and found that nearly half of all agents sold one home or none at all that year.

So do the arithmetic at two closings, and then do it again at zero.

Here's why that changes the answer. A percentage plan costs you nothing in a dead month and a lot in a great one. A fixed-cost plan does the exact opposite. Neither one of those is right or wrong. But only one of them matches the year you're actually about to have.

And ask one more question while you're there. What do I owe in a month where I close nothing.

---

### 4. The cap is priced for somebody you are not yet (4:07 - 4:52)

Most percentage plans have a cap. Once you've paid the brokerage a certain amount in a year, you keep everything after that.

It sounds incredible. And it is incredible, for the agent who hits it.

So ask the question nobody asks. What percentage of the agents in this office hit the cap last year. Not the top team. Everybody.

If they don't know the number, that's an answer. If they do know it, write it down, and then work out what you personally would have to sell to get there.

The cap is a benefit designed for the agent you might become in year four. You're paying for it in year one. That might still be the right trade for you. Just make it on purpose.

---

### 5. The fee they charge your client, that you have to defend and never see (4:52 - 6:01)

This is the one I told you about.

At a lot of firms there's a fee that lands on your client's closing statement. Sometimes it's an administrative fee, sometimes a transaction fee, sometimes a compliance fee.

A report out last month from the Consumer Policy Center estimates those typically run four hundred to six hundred dollars, with a range from two hundred up past two thousand. And I want to be careful here, because that report says plainly it's built largely on anecdotal evidence from agent interviews. So it's an estimate, not a measurement.

But here's the part that matters for you, and the number isn't the point.

Your client is going to see that line at the closing table. And they're going to turn to you and ask what it is. You are the person who answers for it. Not the brokerage. You.

And not a dollar of it reaches you.

That's not a cost to your wallet. That's a cost to your reputation, on a charge you didn't set and can't explain.

So ask two things. Do you charge one. And what does it pay for.

---

### 6. Make them define support, because the word means nothing (6:01 - 6:53)

Every brokerage in America says full support. It's on every recruiting page in the country and it's meaningless, because nobody ever defines it.

So define it for them.

Ask exactly this. It's Saturday at four o'clock. My inspection just fell apart and my buyer is crying in the driveway. Who do I call, and what's the number.

You want a name and a phone number. Not a team. Not a hotline. Not a chat channel.

There was a study out last year that interviewed ten agents who had recently left their brokerages. One of them said he might have talked to his broker ten times in thirteen years. Now, that's ten people, and that's one person's experience, so don't take it as a statistic.

I'm using it because I've had some version of that same conversation on my show more times than I can count.

---

### 7. Available training and attendable training are different things (6:53 - 7:29)

Everybody's going to show you a training calendar.

Ask for the last ninety days instead. The real one. What actually happened, not what's scheduled.

Then look at the times. If new agent training runs Tuesday at ten in the morning, that's the exact hour a working agent is with a client or on the phone. It's on the calendar, and you will never once attend it.

And ask who teaches it. If it's a producing agent giving up an hour of their week, that's real and it's rare. If it's a video library, you already have YouTube.

---

### 8. Read the mentor program like a contract, because it is one (7:29 - 8:15)

Almost every firm has a mentor program for new agents, and almost nobody reads the terms.

Three questions.

Who is my mentor, by name. How many other new agents does that person have right now. And what do they take from my deals, and for how many deals.

That last one usually comes back as a cut of your first two or three closings, on top of the split. Which is completely fair if it's a real mentor who picks up the phone. It's expensive if it's a name on an org chart.

So ask to talk to that person before you sign. Twenty minutes. And if you can't get twenty minutes with your mentor while they're still recruiting you, picture what week three looks like.

---

### 9. Find out what you own (8:15 - 9:11)

Here's the one you won't feel for years.

Everything you're about to build, ask where it lives. Your client database. Your reviews. Your listing photos. Your website. The email address you're about to print on a thousand business cards.

I had a top producer on my show a few years back, Sara DeWulf, and she'd switched firms. Her testimonials lived on a platform her old brokerage paid for. She lost all of them. Thirteen years in this business, and she looked at her Google page and had eleven reviews.

She rebuilt it. She's got hundreds now. But she rebuilt it from zero, in year fourteen.

So before you collect your very first review, ask where it's going to live and whether you can export it. Same question for your database.

Get that one in writing too.

And if somebody gets vague on you when you ask that question, that's worth a conversation. Link's below.

---

### 10. Read the exit before you sign the entrance (9:11 - 10:00)

In the independent contractor agreement, find three things.

What happens to a deal that's under contract on the day I leave. What do I owe after I'm gone. And is there a fee or a referral cut taken out of my pipeline.

If you don't understand a paragraph, have somebody read it who does. This is a contract, and you're a professional now.

Then do the thing almost nobody does. Ask for two agents who joined in the last year and are still there, and call them.

And then go find somebody who left, and call that person too. Nobody's going to hand you that name. Your state license database is public, so look at who moved, and call. Worst case you spend five minutes with somebody who has no reason to sell you anything.

---

### CLOSE (10:00 - 11:35)

So, back to where we started.

The split is the least important number in this conversation, and it's the only one anybody is going to lead with.

Here's what I'd actually do. Take these ten and turn them into one email. Send the same email to three brokerages, not one. Three. And make one of them a firm you're fairly sure you'd never join, because that's the one that shows you what normal looks like.

You have no baseline right now. That's not a knock on you, you got your license on Tuesday. But you're about to sign something without knowing what the market even offers, and one hour of asking fixes that permanently.

Then bring me what comes back. That's the link below. Fifteen minutes, no charge, and I'm not selling you anything on that call. Bring me the three fee schedules and I'll tell you what I see in them.

If one of the three turns out to be my firm, terrific. If it doesn't, you still made this decision on information instead of a feeling, and almost nobody does that.

Last thing, and I think it's what new agents most need to hear. This is not a marriage. NAR puts the median agent at about six years with their current firm, so most people move at some point. Your first brokerage is a year-one decision, not a life decision.

So don't pick a logo. Don't pick because your license instructor mentioned them. And don't pick because the office was nice.

Ask the ten questions.

The split is the last one.

## Production Note

**Rule 7 exception is deliberate and it is logged in the frontmatter.** Every long-form request has to answer "why isn't this two videos," and the answer here is that the completeness is the product. A new licensee wants one artifact they can run in a single sitting before their first recruiting appointment. Item 4 alone is a shrug; items 1 through 10 in order are a decision.

**This is a seated or single-location piece, not a walk-and-talk.** Ten minutes of moving handheld is unwatchable and there's no pattern interrupt doctrine for long form. Shoot it in one place with one camera and let the chapters do the pacing work that the selfie stick does in short form.

**Two open loops carry the retention, and they have to be paid off exactly where promised.** The cold open promises number five costs the most and number nine you won't feel for six years. Item 5 opens with "this is the one I told you about," and item 9 opens with "here's the one you won't feel for years." Those callbacks are the long-form version of the 15-second re-hook. If an edit moves an item, move the tease with it.

**The ownership disclosure is not a legal nicety, it's the strongest move in the script.** A brokerage president saying "I own one of these, discount me accordingly, I'm not pitching you" in the first thirty seconds is Family 3, the Defector, and it buys the credibility that carries all ten items. **Deliver it flat and fast, no apology in the voice.** Do not move it later and do not soften it into "full disclosure."

**The four asks, and the one rule that keeps them from killing the video.** D.J. added the scheduling CTA on 2026-08-19. The asks sit at roughly 1:05, 3:10, 9:05, and 10:25, and they escalate: a plant, then two attached to a specific problem the viewer just learned they have, then the real one.

**The rule: every ask is attached to the thing said immediately before it, never to the brand.** Ask two lands the second after the viewer learns some brokerages won't email a fee schedule, so the offer is "bring me the lists," not "come talk about joining." Ask three lands the second after the ownership item, so the offer is "somebody got vague on you." An ask that could be lifted out and pasted anywhere in the video is a commercial. An ask that only makes sense where it sits is a next step. **If any of these gets moved in the edit, rewrite its wording to match its new neighbor or cut it.**

**The cold open had to change, and this is the part that matters most.** The no-ask draft said "I'm not pitching you today." That line cannot survive four asks, and leaving it in would have made this a bait and switch, which is worse than a straight pitch. The replacement declares the link in the first minute, says out loud that some viewers will want to ask about his firm and most won't, and gives the ten questions away unconditionally. **The honesty in the first minute is what buys the ask at 10:25.** Do not soften it, do not move it later, and do not cut it for time.

**"No charge, and I'm not selling you anything on that call" is a promise, not copy.** If the call turns into a recruiting pitch, the video stops working and the comments will say so. The call is a real review of what the three brokerages sent back, and the firm comes up only if the agent raises it. That is a booking-flow requirement, not a delivery note.

**Build a dedicated Calendly event type before this posts.** The existing link in the repo is `calendly.com/joinkale/ai-strategy-session`, and that framing is wrong for this audience by a mile. A brand new licensee is not booking an AI strategy session. Make one called something like "Brokerage Questions, 15 minutes," set it to 15 minutes to match what's spoken, and point it at a landing page that repeats the ten questions so the click confirms the promise. **The spoken script says fifteen minutes four times. If the event type is thirty, change the script or change the event.**

**Close routing.** Bookings off this video need their own source tag so it can be measured against the ten Shorts and against every other CTA D.J. runs. Per the Calendly phone-capture issue already logged, confirm the phone question is set to required on this event type before the first booking lands.

**Do not put the ask in the ten Shorts.** They slot into the Broker Problems rotation, which is ungated and un-pitched by standard, and the reach penalty on overt recruiting content in D.J.'s own Facebook data is the reason. The Shorts point at the long form; the long form carries the ask.

**No brokerage is named as wrong anywhere in this script, and none may be added.** Ship test: could a viewer name ONE company with confidence from anything said here. Currently no. Item 5 is the one an edit will want to sharpen with a name. Refuse it.

**The concessions are load-bearing and there are four of them.** Item 1 ("a big brand can buy you a real training system"), item 3 ("neither one is right or wrong"), item 4 ("that might still be the right trade"), item 8 ("completely fair if it's a real mentor"). Each one keeps the video from being a man arguing against brokerages, which is what it becomes the moment one of them gets cut for time. **They are the cheapest thing to cut and the most expensive thing to lose.**

**Two source limits are spoken out loud, on purpose.** Item 5 says the report is built on anecdotal evidence. Item 6 says ten people is not a statistic. Both lines will feel like they weaken the point. They do the opposite: a guy who tells you the limits of his own evidence is the guy you believe on the other nine items, and it makes the video unattackable in the comments. **Do not cut either line.**

**The Chicago numbers need a national swap if this posts to the main YouTube channel.** Item 1 uses the Illinois figure. For a national cut, replace with the viewer's own state count and drop the two names, or keep Chicago and accept that a national viewer takes it as an illustration. **Recommendation: keep Chicago.** The specificity is worth more than the universality, and "there are fourteen hundred brokerages in one city" makes the point everywhere.

**Courtesy heads-up on the two names, same requirement as BP-005.** Greg Pekarsky and Matt Laricy are named positively and nothing critical is said about either man or his firm. D.J. runs a competing brokerage, so they hear it from him first. Do not tag either one in the post.

**Item 10 says "your state license database is public."** True in Illinois (IDFPR, data.illinois.gov). It is broadly true nationally but not universally, and the script hedges by saying "your state." Do not upgrade it to "every state."

**Ten Shorts come out of this cut, one per item.** Each item is written to stand alone with its own hook and its own close. That is the real economics of the ten minutes: the long form is the asset, the ten Shorts are the distribution. Log each Short against its own bank entry, not against this script.

**No AI music prompt on this one, and that is deliberate.** Every walk-and-talk script in this repo ships with a CapCut music prompt because a moving 60-second video needs a bed. A ten-minute seated piece does not; a loop under ten minutes of talking becomes maddening by minute three and it makes an authority video feel like an ad. **Music only under the cold open and the end card, if at all.** The ten Shorts pulled from this cut each get their own prompt when they are built, per `docs/ai-music-prompts.md`.

**Comment duty applies and it is heavier here than on a Short.** A ten-minute video for new licensees will produce comments from people about to make the decision. Those are the highest-intent comments this brand generates. Flag every Chicago one into Close.

## Why the ask is written the way it is

D.J. directed the scheduling CTA on 2026-08-19, replacing a no-ask draft. This section records what changed and what the change costs, so the tradeoff is visible rather than assumed.

**What it costs.** The no-ask version was credible partly because it never asked for anything, and that version of the video is the one that gets recommended by other people in comment sections. Four asks make it a marketing asset instead of a neutral one, and a share of viewers will discount all ten items on that basis. That cost is real and it is not recoverable by better wording.

**What buys it back.** Three things, and all three are load-bearing.

1. **The disclosure moved to the front and got bigger.** The conflict and the link are both declared in the first minute, before a single one of the ten items. Nobody can feel tricked at 10:25 by something they were told at 0:40.
2. **The ten questions are given away unconditionally and completely.** Nothing is held back for the call. The video is worth the eleven minutes to somebody who never clicks, which is the same design rule the Value Giveaway pillar runs on.
3. **The ask is for a review of somebody else's paperwork, not for a recruiting conversation.** "Bring me the three fee schedules and I'll tell you what I see" is a service that is useful whether or not the viewer ever considers his firm, and the script says so out loud.

**What would break it.** Naming a competitor as bad anywhere in the ten items. Adding a fifth ask. Moving any ask off the item it is attached to. Turning the booked call into a pitch. Any one of those converts the previous ten minutes into an ad retroactively, which is the exact failure the Broker Problems standard describes for the Permission Slip flavor.

**The measurable version of this bet.** The no-ask draft is in git history. If this cut underperforms badly on watch time or the comments turn on the CTA, the alternative is not a softer ask, it is the ungated video plus a gated document of the ten questions on Instagram and Facebook under the Rule 4 value-exchange exception, with the booking link living on the document instead of in the video. That keeps the hand-raise and takes the ask out of the eleven minutes entirely. **Register the keyword in [`../../data/keyword-registry.md`](../../data/keyword-registry.md) if it goes that way.**

## Council Review

**Why it should work:**

- **Hook mechanism.** The cold open does two things at once that normally fight each other. It disqualifies the speaker ("I run a brokerage, discount me") and then immediately makes him the only trustworthy person in the room, because nobody else the viewer talks to this month is going to declare their interest. Family 7, Forbidden, delivered through Family 3, the Defector. A new licensee has been handed nothing but sales pitches for two weeks. This is the first thing that sounds like it isn't one.
- **Retention.** Long form lives or dies on open loops, and there are two planted at 0:40 that don't pay off until 5:00 and 8:45. Everything between them is a numbered list, which is the single most retention-friendly structure on YouTube because the viewer always knows where they are and how much is left.
- **Search.** This is the rare piece of D.J.'s content with real durable search intent behind it. "How to choose a real estate brokerage" gets typed by every new licensee in America, forever, and almost every result is written by a brokerage. A ten-minute answer from a broker who says he's a broker and then doesn't pitch is a genuinely differentiated result.
- **Economics.** Ten Shorts fall out of one shoot. At two Broker Problems a week, this one filming session feeds five weeks of that lane.

**The dissent, and it's a real one:** the audience for this video does not convert to Kale. Brand new licensees are the lowest-value recruiting target there is. Roughly half of them will be out of the business inside two years, they close almost nothing in year one, and the AMP program (Rea's lane) is the only place they'd land anyway.

**Two answers.** First, the honest one: this video is not a recruiting asset, it's a search-and-authority asset, and it should be judged on views, comments, and whether it still gets found in eighteen months. Second, the less obvious one: the ten questions in this video are the same ten questions a **five-year agent** has never asked either. A meaningful share of the audience will be experienced agents watching a video labeled for beginners, which is exactly what happens to every "beginner" video that's actually good. Those are the viewers who convert. **Do not retarget the script at them.** Writing it for the new agent is what makes the experienced one watch it without feeling sold to.

**The second dissent:** ten minutes violates the strategy's entire short-form focus, and D.J.'s own data says long form is where his content goes to be ignored. Fair. The mitigation is that the ten Shorts are the real distribution plan and the long form is the artifact they point at. **If the video underperforms on YouTube and the Shorts do well, that is a success, not a failure.** Judge the Shorts.

**Recruiting test (guardrail 3), rerun after the CTA was added.** A good agent watches a brokerage owner declare his conflict and his link in the first minute, give away ten questions that make his own recruiting conversation harder, state the limits of his own sources twice, name two competitors' principals as proof, and then ask for fifteen minutes to read somebody else's paperwork with him. Respect still goes up on every one of those, and the ask is the least of them rather than the point of them.

**It passes, but by a narrower margin than the no-ask draft, and the margin lives entirely in the first minute.** The test fails the moment a viewer feels the ten items were bait. What prevents that is the declaration at 0:40, the fact that nothing is held back for the call, and the promise that the call is not a pitch. **Two of those three are copy and one of them is an operational commitment.** If the booked call turns into a recruiting conversation, this video fails the test retroactively and the comments are where it shows up.

**The third dissent, and it's the sharpest one:** four asks in eleven minutes is a lot for an audience of brand new licensees who mostly cannot join anything useful yet. The counter is that asks two and three cost almost nothing because they are attached to a problem the viewer just acquired, and the plant at 1:05 is doing credibility work rather than conversion work. **The one genuinely at risk is the plant.** If watch-time drops off a cliff in the first ninety seconds, cut the plant and keep the disclosure, and let the link live only in the close and the description.
