# Comment below is why fewer people see your posts

> **WOW: Tactical specificity (1). The behaviour is named as the literal words on the screen, the harm is a published platform policy rather than an opinion, and the fix is one line deleted from the next caption.**

> **Strongest Line:** "You didn't lose the comment by not asking. You were losing the post by asking."

## Spoken Script (22-30 seconds, 70 words)

**THE COW**
> Stop writing comment below on your posts. It's the reason fewer people are seeing them.

**WHO PROFITS**
> Engagement coaching sells comment counts because they're easy to sell.

**THE RECEIPT**
> Meta's own policy calls that engagement bait and cuts the post's reach. Live since 2017.

**THE SWAP**
> Delete the line. Replace it with one sentence an agent would actually send another agent.

**LOOP-BACK**
> You didn't lose the comment by not asking. You were losing the post by asking.

## Production Note

- **Shape 1, imperative plus harm, and the harm is in the same breath.** "Stop writing comment below" alone is half a hook. The sentence does not end until the reach is named.
- **Pattern interrupt: Gesture-On-Beat**, palm-to-camera on "Stop." The cheat sheet pairs it with Swap/List and it is the one move that reads on a mute scroll.
- **Do not write STOP in capitals.** Captions build from the audio; the emphasis is delivery.
- **The irony is the point and it must not be said out loud.** This script asks for nothing, and if it works it will out-earn the posts that beg. Let the comments make that observation themselves. A line pointing at it turns the take into a brag.
- **Meta is named as the source, not the villain.** The villain is the coaching that sells comment count as the metric. That keeps this at heat 4. Naming a coach or a program would be heat 5 and is banned outright.
- **Lane note:** this is also the repo's own standing rule (`docs/caption-and-hashtag-strategy.md` bans engagement asks on every platform), which makes it a position D.J. already lives by rather than one he adopted for a video.

**This take is policy-anchored, not stat-anchored** (`stat_anchored: false`), which Rule 9.3's stat-light fallback permits. A first-party platform policy stating the demotion outright is a stronger receipt for this specific claim than any third-party correlation number would be.

**Re-verification note, and a correction to the brief.** The Aug 17 brief paired the Meta policy with *"Metricool's 2026 Social Media Study confirms posts built around a 'send this to someone' moment now outperform posts built around asking for a comment."* That claim does not survive checking. Metricool's 2026 Social Media Study (1,059,949 accounts, 39,762,999 posts) reports reach, impression and posting-frequency trends by platform. It does not compare engagement-solicitation methods, and it contains no finding of that shape. The citation was dropped rather than softened.

Beat 3 therefore runs on Meta's policy alone, which is also the only source that actually supports the hook's causal claim. Note that Rule 1 forbids stacking two findings into one spoken breath regardless, so the brief's version would have needed splitting even if it had held.

**Who-profits support:** category-level. No coach, program, or product is named.

## Social Media

### LinkedIn

The "comment below" line at the bottom of your post is the reason fewer people are seeing it.

This is not a theory about the algorithm. Meta published the policy in December 2017 and it is still enforced. The Transparency Center defines engagement bait as posts that explicitly request engagement, including comments, for purposes other than a specific call to action. The original announcement is blunt about the consequence: accounts using these tactics should expect reach on those posts to decrease, and accounts that keep doing it see more significant drops overall.

So the ask isn't a neutral little nudge at the end of a caption. It is the thing suppressing the post it's attached to.

The reason it persists is that comment count is the easiest metric to sell. A coach can put it on a slide. Nobody has to explain what a comment actually bought you.

Delete the line from your next caption. Replace it with one sentence you'd actually send another agent unprompted. That's the version that travels, because a send is worth more than a comment and it can't be begged for.

You didn't lose the comment by not asking. You were losing the post by asking.

## Council Review

**Convened:** Kane, Youshaei, GaryVee, Hormozi, Eric Simon. **Witnesses:** Heath, Berger.

### Spoken scroll-stop variants

- **A. Default, Family 9 Swap/List.** "Stop writing comment below on your posts. It's the reason fewer people are seeing them."
- **B. Shape 5, paradox verdict.** "Every time you ask for a comment, you pay for it in reach."
- **C. Family 7, Forbidden.** "Your coach told you to ask for the comment. Meta has been demoting that since 2017."

### The room

**Kane.** A is a rule. B is a hook point. "You pay for it in reach" is nine words, it's a paradox, and the viewer's first reaction is that it's wrong, which is the loop opening by itself. A is clearer and B is stickier. That's a real test, not a preference.

**Youshaei.** The zero-second frame matters more here than on most takes, because this topic looks like every other social-media-tips video in the feed. Palm-to-camera on the first syllable is the whole visual differentiator. If there's a phone in frame showing an actual caption with the line highlighted, better. Go physical.

**GaryVee.** Right, but be honest about what this is. It's a platform-mechanics tip wearing a take's clothes. It's true, it's useful, it'll do numbers with the agents who post. It's not going to move anyone who doesn't post, and that's most of them.

**Hormozi.** Disagree on the value. The reward is unusually high for the runtime because deleting a line is free and the upside is distribution on everything they post from now on. That's the best value-to-effort ratio in the whole lane.

**Eric Simon.** The recognition is strong. Every agent has typed that line and felt slightly gross doing it. Where it gets sendable is the second half of the swap, the sentence one agent would actually send another. That's the part people screenshot.

**Heath.** Concrete in the strict sense: the behaviour is named as the literal words on the screen, not as a category like "engagement bait." That defeats the Curse of Knowledge, because the viewer does not have to translate the abstraction into their own Tuesday. They already typed it.

**Berger.** Practical Value, which is the most reliably shared of the six and the least glamorous. It will get sent privately more than it gets commented on, which is the correct outcome and also, pleasingly, the argument the video is making.

### Why it works

**Hook (Heath):** names the literal behaviour rather than the category, so no translation is required. **Share driver (Berger):** Practical Value, delivered as a free fix with compounding upside. **Retention (MrBeast):** the imperative occupies second zero and the receipt arrives by second twelve, before skepticism can form.

### The dissent to A/B

**Kane holds that the default hook is a rule rather than a hook point.** Test A against B ("every time you ask for a comment, you pay for it in reach") on equal cold spend, judged on 3-second retention and sends. B is the shorter, flippier construction and the whole force is in the flip, so if it wins, the paradox shape becomes the Monday default.

**GaryVee's separate note, not a test:** if this underperforms, the cause is audience fit rather than craft. Do not rewrite the hook. Check whether the posting cohort is big enough to be worth a Monday slot.
