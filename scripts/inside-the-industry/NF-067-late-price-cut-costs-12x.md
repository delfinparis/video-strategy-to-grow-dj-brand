# A Week Eight Price Cut Costs Twelve Times a Week Two Price Cut

> **WOW: Named-stakes market intelligence with a behavioral turn. REcolorado's Q2 2026 data across 11 Greater Denver Metro counties shows listings that cut price between day 8 and day 14 averaged a $2,321 reduction, while listings that waited until day 50 to 56 averaged $28,278, which the report itself calls more than a 12-times increase. The insight an agent cannot get from the headline is that the gap is not the market moving, it is six weeks of nobody wanting to be the one who brings up price. The close converts that into a single line in the listing agreement, agreed on day one while the correction is still cheap.**

**Pillar:** Market Intelligence | **Hook Family:** Named Stakes | **Heat:** 3 | **Avatars:** Listing agents everywhere first, Chicagoland sellers' agents second
**Filming:** Walk and talk. If there is a price-reduced rider or a sign that has obviously been up a while on the route, film it.
**Pattern interrupt:** The Whip. Hold on a for-sale sign or a price-reduced rider for about a second, then whip the camera to your face on "Twelve times." The sign is the only B-roll this script needs and it is free on almost any walk.

## Script (~58 seconds, walk and talk)

### HOOK (0:00-0:11)
Twelve times. That's how much more it costs your seller to cut the price in week eight than in week two. And it isn't the market doing it.

### STORY (0:11-0:38)
REcolorado just published Denver's Q2 numbers. Listings that cut between day eight and fourteen averaged two thousand three hundred twenty-one dollars. The ones that waited until day fifty to fifty-six averaged twenty-eight thousand two hundred seventy-eight.

Nobody wakes up on day fifty and decides to lose twenty-six grand. Week two shows up, showings are thin, and you don't want to be the agent who brings up price already. So you give it one more week. Then one more. You're being kind. That's what kind costs.

### PAYOFF (0:38-0:58)
The window is tighter right now. Active inventory has held above one point one million for seven straight weeks. Buyers have somewhere else to go.

Here's what you do now. Before you take the next listing, put the date in the agreement. No offer by day fourteen, the number moves. In writing, on day one, while it's cheap.

You're not deciding whether to cut. You're deciding what it costs.

## Data Source

- **Claim:** "REcolorado just published Denver's Q2 numbers. Listings that cut between day eight and fourteen averaged two thousand three hundred twenty-one dollars. The ones that waited until day fifty to fifty-six averaged twenty-eight thousand two hundred seventy-eight."
  - Source: Colorado Horse Property, "Denver's Q2 2026 Price Reductions and Seller Concessions," published 2026-07-29 ([link](https://coloradohorseproperty.com/blog/denvers-q2-2026-price-reductions-and-seller-concessions/)). Underlying data is REcolorado's analysis of closed residential listings across 11 Greater Denver Metro counties, April through June 2026. Report language: "Listings on the market for 8 to 14 days averaged a $2,321 price cut. By 50 to 56 days, the average price cut had grown to $28,278, more than a 12-times increase."
  - Status: confirmed (figures and day brackets quoted directly from the report)

- **Claim:** "Twelve times. That's how much more it costs your seller to cut the price in week eight than in week two."
  - Source: The "more than a 12-times increase" characterization is the report's own, not a calculation of ours. Day 8-14 is week two; day 50-56 is week eight. Stated as a multiple rather than as rounded dollar figures specifically so the hook carries no rounding (universal Rule 1, NF hook rigor).
  - Status: confirmed

- **Claim:** "Active inventory has held above one point one million for seven straight weeks."
  - Source: Realtor.com data dated 2026-08-06, reported in Carrington Wholesale, "Weekly Housing Headlines," 2026-08-07 ([link](https://www.carringtonwholesale.com/2026/08/07/weekly-housing-headlines-91/)): active inventory held above 1.1 million properties for the seventh consecutive week.
  - Status: confirmed

- **Claim (context, not narrated on camera):** "20% of listings took a price cut in July."
  - Source: Realtor.com data dated 2026-08-03, via the same Carrington roundup: the share of listings with a price cut reached 20% in July, nearly even with last year's level. Used in the LinkedIn caption only. Deliberately kept out of the spoken script because "nearly even with last year" undercuts an urgency read, and using it on camera without that qualifier would overstate the news.
  - Status: confirmed, with the flat-year-over-year qualifier carried wherever it appears

- **Claim:** "Nobody wakes up on day fifty and decides to lose twenty-six grand... you don't want to be the agent who brings up price already."
  - Source: D.J. Paris's editorial framing of the behavioral mechanism. The data establishes the correlation between time on market and cut size; it does not establish the cause. This beat is explicitly labeled as a read, not a finding. The $26,000 figure is the arithmetic difference between the two published averages ($28,278 minus $2,321 = $25,957), spoken as "twenty-six grand."
  - Status: editorial framing, arithmetic verified

- **Scope caveat (important):** The dollar figures are Denver metro only, and the script says "Denver" on camera so no viewer mistakes them for national numbers. The national inventory datapoint is what extends the relevance outside Colorado. Do not present the $2,321 and $28,278 figures as national averages in any repurpose, carousel, or clip caption.

- **Causation caveat:** The report shows that cuts taken later were larger. It does not show that an early cut prevents a later large one. Homes still sitting at day 50 are plausibly the most mispriced at list. The script's claim is limited to what the data supports, that the correction gets more expensive the longer it waits, and the close is a timing mechanism rather than a promise of a specific saving.

**Why it should work:**
- Hook mechanism (Heath): "Twelve times" opens the gap before any number closes it, and the third sentence names what the answer is not ("it isn't the market doing it") without revealing what it is. Curse of Knowledge check passes because no MLS literacy, no local knowledge, and no prior script are required to follow the first line.
- Share/save driver (Berger/Hormozi): high-arousal anxiety attached to a specific dollar figure the agent can picture on their own listing, plus Social Currency for the sender, who looks like the agent who reads the data. Valuable-virality check passes, an agent can retell this in one sentence ("cut early, the cut is small") and D.J.'s point survives intact. Hormozi signs off on the close because the takeaway is a form change that costs nothing and takes thirty seconds.
- Retention move (MrBeast): the turn lands at 0:24, right at the decay point, and it flips the register from data to behavior so the second half is a different kind of watching than the first. The two dollar figures are held apart by six weeks of narrative rather than delivered as a pair, so the second number is a payoff rather than a footnote.

**The dissent (your next A/B test):** Byron Lazine and GaryVee both object that the anchor is one metro. Byron's version: the national story this week is seven straight weeks above 1.1 million and a 20% July price-cut share, and leading with Denver dollars caps the reach of a piece that could own a national headline. Chris Do counters that the Denver dollars are the only thing in the script that makes an agent feel it, and a national percentage feels like a chart. **The experiment:** cut a second version that opens on the national inventory number and demotes Denver to the proof, then compare. Hypothesis: the national open travels wider on reach, the Denver open wins on saves and sends per view. If the national open also wins on saves, stop anchoring market-mechanics scripts to single-metro data.
