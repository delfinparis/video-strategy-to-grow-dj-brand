# Compass Just Said the Quiet Part Out Loud

> **WOW: Surprising-source statistic + permission-slip take -- Compass Chief Economist Mike Simonsen, who in December 2025 published a 2026 outlook calling for "the most balanced housing market in years" with existing home sales up 4.25-5%, told Inman on 2026-04-30 that the year has been "a little disappointing." That tonal shift from a brokerage-paid economist -- whose job is to keep agents and sellers calibrated to a positive narrative -- is the permission slip every honest agent needs to reset seller pricing conversations. The take: stop quoting December's forecast in May. Compass's own chief economist just told you not to.**

### LinkedIn (PRIMARY)
**Caption:**
Compass's chief economist just told the industry that 2026 has been, quote, "a little disappointing."

That is not a competitor talking. That is their own economist. And it changes the conversation you should be having with every seller this week.

Here is the contrast that matters.

December 2025. Compass publishes its 2026 outlook. The headline: "Most Balanced Housing Market in Years." Existing home sales projected up 4.25 to 5 percent. National prices up half a point. Inventory up 5 percent. Mortgage rates around 6.4. Mike Simonsen, the chief economist, on the record: "The market is shifting toward a new era where incomes rise faster than home prices and the deep freeze of the last few years begins to thaw."

Four months later, the same Mike Simonsen tells Inman: "Overall, it's been a little disappointing."

That is not a small revision. That is a publicly traded brokerage admitting in print that the year is undershooting their own forecast. And it is the part of the story most agents are about to miss in the noise of all the other industry headlines this week.

Here is why this matters more than the Inman article frames it.

Brokerages do not pay chief economists to be pessimistic. They pay them to keep agents and sellers calibrated to a positive narrative. The job description includes morale management. So when the economist who works for one of the biggest brokerages in the country uses the word "disappointing" on the record, that word has to clear two filters before it reaches the page. The economist's own caution. And the brokerage's marketing alignment. It cleared both. That tells you the gap between the December forecast and April reality is wider than "a little disappointing" actually sounds.

Here is what to do with this in your business this week.

Your seller is still pricing on December's forecast. They watched the year-end recaps. They saw the headlines about a "balanced market." They have an anchor in their head that says "this is the year inventory finally gets absorbed and prices firm up." Your buyer is still spooked by January's headlines about rates and affordability. Both clients are using outdated mental models.

The chief economist just gave you the language to update both.

For the seller: "Compass's chief economist published a forecast in December calling for the most balanced market in years. Last week he said the year has been disappointing. The market is moving slower than the December forecast assumed. Your price has to reflect today's market, not last December's outlook." You are not being negative. You are quoting their preferred brokerage's own economist.

For the buyer: "The market the headlines describe is not the market that has actually played out. The chief economist of one of the biggest brokerages in the country called this year disappointing on the record. There is more leverage in the room right now than buyers think. Let's go look."

Stop selling December's market in May. The chief economist just gave you permission. Use it.
