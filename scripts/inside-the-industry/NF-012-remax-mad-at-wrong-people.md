## Data Source

- **Claim:** "Eight hundred and eighty million dollars just went to RE/MAX shareholders."
  - Source: Real Brokerage investor announcement, "Real to Acquire RE/MAX, Creating a Leading Technology-Enabled Global Real Estate Platform," 2026-04-27 ([link](https://investors.onereal.com/news/news-details/2026/Real-to-Acquire-REMAX-Creating-a-Leading-Technology-Enabled-Global-Real-Estate-Platform/default.aspx)). Deal value: $880M total consideration. RE/MAX Holdings (NYSE: RMAX) shareholders receive $13.80/share or 5.15 shares of the combined company, with aggregate cash capped at $60-80M (per Wolf Street, [link](https://wolfstreet.com/2026/04/27/two-more-imploded-real-estate-brokerage-stocks-tie-the-knot-remax-85-from-peak-real-brokerage-70/)).
  - Note: Strictly, the $880M flows to RMAX *shareholders* (public float, institutional holders, and insider/founder equity such as RIHI Inc.), not "RE/MAX corporate" as a legal entity. The script uses "RE/MAX shareholders" in the hook for accuracy and "RE/MAX corporate" elsewhere as colloquial shorthand for the leadership and insider-owners who controlled and benefited from the sale. The structural point -- agents are not on the cap table -- is correct either way.
  - Status: confirmed

- **Claim:** "A twelve-year-old cap-and-stock company just bought the fifty-year-old franchise icon."
  - Source: Real Brokerage Inc. was incorporated in 2014; brokerage operations launched 2014-2015; went public via reverse merger in 2020. Cap-and-stock model is well-documented in Real's investor materials. RE/MAX was founded in Denver in 1973 (53 years old as of 2026). Spoken script rounds to "twelve" and "fifty" for cadence -- defensible since the qualitative claim is "young upstart vs decades-old icon," not a precise anniversary.
  - Status: confirmed qualitatively (consistent with NF-009's "barely a decade old" / "around fifty years" framing)

- **Claim:** "That's not a merger. That's a verdict. The franchise model lost."
  - Source: D.J.'s editorial framing. The structural argument: in M&A, the direction of consolidation reflects which model the market believes in. When the largest, oldest, most-recognized representative of Model A sells itself to a much younger representative of Model B, the deal itself is evidence about which model has the long-run advantage. In this case, the largest legacy franchise (RE/MAX) sold itself to a structurally different competitor (cap-and-stock) -- a deal direction the industry called surprising on announcement (HousingWire: "'Didn't see that coming'"). D.J.'s framing reads that surprise as verdict.
  - Status: editorial opinion, clearly framed as such; structural reasoning grounded in deal direction

- **Claim:** "RE/MAX corporate saw it coming and took the exit."
  - Source: D.J.'s framing of seller motivation. Inferential, not from a specific RE/MAX executive quote. Defensible because: corporate boards of public companies do not sell at $13.80/share (well below the 2017 peak that put RMAX 85% off all-time-high per Wolf Street) unless leadership concludes the standalone model has limited upside. The fact-pattern -- standalone trajectory weak, accept buyout below peak -- is consistent with leadership reading the model as no longer competitive. D.J. names that read directly.
  - Status: editorial inference, defensible from public deal data

- **Claim:** "Not one agent was on the call. Not one agent got a check."
  - Source: D.J.'s structural framing. Individual sales agents at RE/MAX-affiliated franchisees are independent contractors, not RE/MAX Holdings shareholders by virtue of their affiliation. Unless a specific agent personally owns RMAX stock, they receive no consideration from the deal. Standard franchise model: corporate parent monetizes the brand; affiliated agents do not participate in equity outcomes.
  - Status: structurally accurate; clearly framed as D.J.'s reframe

- **Claim:** "RE/MAX agents are publicly mad at Real for the buyout."
  - Source: HousingWire, "'Didn't see that coming': Real estate brokers react to Real-REMAX deal," 2026-04-28 ([link](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/real-remax-deal-reaction/)); Inman, "'A Game Changer': Agents React To $880 Million Real-REMAX Deal," 2026-04-28 ([link](https://www.inman.com/2026/04/28/a-game-changer-agents-react-to-880m-real-remax-deal/)). RE/MAX agent quoted in Inman: "I love being a RE/MAX agent. I take pride in it. I have no desire to work under the Real signage but will have to wait and see what they have to offer." Public reactions concentrate concern on Real / brand change / integration -- not on the seller's decision to sell.
  - Status: confirmed via published reactions

- **Editorial framing:** "Aim your anger up. Not sideways." / "the model lost."
  - Status: D.J.'s reframe. Two load-bearing structural facts under the rhetoric: (1) the deal flows to shareholders and not to affiliated agents; (2) the deal direction (upstart buys legacy) is the market's verdict on relative model strength.

- **Open question for D.J. before filming:** Tone calibration. The script names "the franchise model lost" as the why-corporate-sold answer. RE/MAX corporate-aligned commenters and franchise-model loyalists will push back hard. D.J. should be ready to defend two claims: (1) the deal direction itself is evidence; (2) accepting a price 85% off all-time-high (per Wolf Street) is consistent with leadership viewing standalone upside as limited. If D.J. wants a softer landing, swap "the model lost" for "the model stopped winning" -- same point, less absolute.

### LinkedIn (PRIMARY)
**Caption:**
RE/MAX agents are mad at Real Brokerage for the 880-million-dollar buyout.

They are mad at the wrong people.

Real did not take RE/MAX. RE/MAX corporate sold RE/MAX.

Here is the part missing from every "what does this mean for agents" piece I have read this week.

A twelve-year-old cap-and-stock company just bought the fifty-year-old franchise icon for 880 million dollars. The deal direction is the story. When the biggest, oldest, most-recognized representative of one business model sells itself to a much younger representative of a structurally different business model, the deal itself is evidence about which model has the long-run advantage.

Translation: this is not a merger. It is a verdict. The franchise model lost. And RE/MAX corporate -- the leadership team and insider holders who watched the standalone trajectory and accepted a price well below the 2017 peak -- saw it coming and took the exit.

Now look at where the 880 million dollars went.

It went to RE/MAX Holdings shareholders. Insiders. Institutional holders. Public float. The cash portion is capped at 60 to 80 million across the entire shareholder base. The rest is paid in stock of the combined company. Either way, the people who got paid are the people who already held RMAX equity. Not the agents who built the brand on the ground for two decades.

Every fee you paid. Every royalty your office paid. Every yard sign. Every conference badge. Every sponsored event. All of it built the 880-million-dollar valuation. RE/MAX corporate cashed it in. You got a new owner.

And now agents are posting in groups about Real. About the new signage. About cap-and-stock. About what changes after close.

I get it. The change is real. The uncertainty is real. But the anger is pointed in the wrong direction.

Real did not do this to you. Real wrote a check. Real saw a brand for sale and bought it. That is what buyers do.

The seller decided to sell. The seller signed the papers. The seller is the same leadership team you have trusted for years to grow the brand you affiliated with. The seller is the brand on your business card. And the seller did not pick up the phone to sell because the franchise model was winning. They picked up the phone because the model lost.

So here is the take. Stop being mad at Real. Real wrote a check. Be mad at the people who took it without telling you the brand was for sale -- and without telling you why it was for sale. Because the answer is the part of the story nobody at corporate is going to print on the next franchise renewal packet: the model lost.

Then -- once the anger is aimed at the right people -- decide what you are going to do about it.

The choice is not "stay or go." The choice is whether you keep paying into franchise systems where the corporate parent can sell you to a competitor without your input. Or whether the next brokerage you affiliate with is one where the agents are on the cap table.

That is the question this deal actually puts on the table. Real did not put it there. RE/MAX did.
