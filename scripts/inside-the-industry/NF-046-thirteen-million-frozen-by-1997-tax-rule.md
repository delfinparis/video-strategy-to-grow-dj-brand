> **WOW: Surprising statistic (Rule 0, criterion 4) plus tactical specificity (criterion 1). - **Claim:** "Thirteen million American homeowners are frozen in place." / "Thirteen million think the door's closed."
  - Source: National Association of Realtors research, reported by Realtor.com / AOL, "Why more homeowners owe capital gains tax when they sell," May 31, 2026, and by The Epoch Times, "13 Million US Homeowners Face Potential Capital Gains Taxes: Report," June 10, 2026. Verbatim: "New research by the National Association of Realtors estimates that nationwide about 13.1 million homeowners -- 15% of all owner-occupied households -- already have unrealized capital gains in their homes that exceed the limits allowed for the capital gains tax break." URLs: https://www.aol.com/finance/why-more-homeowners-owe-capital-120204476.html and https://www.theepochtimes.com/business/13-million-us-homeowners-face-potential-capital-gains-taxes-report-6043813
  - Who was measured: U.S. owner-occupied households with unrealized gains exceeding the exclusion.
  - Status: confirmed. Note: the precise figure is NAR's "about 13.1 million." The spoken script uses the round "thirteen million," which understates the true figure rather than inflating it. This is a NAR estimate, NOT a Redfin number. Do not attribute it to Redfin.

- **Claim:** "You can shield two hundred fifty thousand in gains if you're single, five hundred thousand if you're married. Congress set those limits in nineteen ninety-seven and never touched them."
  - Source: Section 121 of the Internal Revenue Code, exclusion set at $250,000 single / $500,000 joint by the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997, never indexed to inflation. Reinforced by NAR, "Congress Writes to Sec. Bessent on Indexing Capital Gains," March 6, 2026, quoting the congressional letter: "Those limits were set in 1997 and have never been adjusted for inflation." URL: https://www.nar.realtor/news/real-estate-news/congress-writes-to-sec-bessent-on-indexing-capital-gains
  - Who was measured: tax code / legislative fact.
  - Status: confirmed (publicly verifiable).

- **Claim (framing):** "frozen in place" / "the answer was stay put" / "they're deciding to stay."
  - Source: This lock-in framing is NAR's own. NAR, "The Capital Gains Cliff Is Coming: How Reform Can Unlock Housing Supply," May 30, 2025, describes a "stay-put penalty" that "freezes inventory": "When an older homeowner decides not to sell, it limits the number of homes on the market." Named-economist support: Joel Berner, senior economist for Realtor.com, in the May 31, 2026 AOL/Realtor.com piece: "if they sell the big home, they get hit with this capital gains tax. So, they're deciding to stay in place." URL: https://www.nar.realtor/taxes/capital-gains/the-capital-gains-cliff-is-coming-how-reform-can-unlock-housing-supply
  - Who was measured: industry/economist characterization of homeowner behavior.
  - Status: confirmed (sourced to named organization and named economist). This is no longer presented as unsupported commentary.

- **Corroborating data (used in social captions, not the spoken script):** Redfin, "California, Hawaii and Massachusetts Have the Highest Share of Homeowners Who Would Benefit if Capital Gains Tax Is Eliminated," published August 4, 2025 (updated August 7, 2025): "More than one in four (25.9%) U.S. homes have gained at least $250,000 in value since the last time they were purchased, while 8% have gained more than $500,000." Median gain at the $250,000 tier: $384,606, a potential tax liability of $20,104 at a 15% rate. URL: https://www.redfin.com/news/capital-gains-2025/
  - Who was measured: U.S. homes with a discernible value gain since last purchase. Note: Redfin measures share of HOMES with $250K+ gains, a different population and measure than NAR's count of households whose gains exceed THEIR exclusion. The captions keep the two sources distinct and do not present Redfin's 25.9% as the origin of NAR's 13.1 million.
  - Status: confirmed (publicly verifiable).

- **Supporting context (not a spoken claim):** NAR supports the bipartisan More Homes on the Market Act, which would update and index the exclusion amounts. Members of the Congressional Real Estate Caucus, including co-chair Mark Alford (R-Mo.), wrote to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on March 6, 2026 urging that capital gains be indexed to inflation. The script does not state a legislative outcome as fact.

- **Claim (commentary):** "plenty owe far less than they think."
  - Source: D.J.'s framing, grounded in general tax mechanics. Taxable gain is reduced by original cost basis, capital improvements, and the exclusion itself, so many longtime owners overestimate their exposure. Presented as opinion, not a statistic.
  - Status: commentary (no quantitative claim made).

- **Verification before filming:** Re-check the NAR 13.1 million figure and any movement on the More Homes on the Market Act between today and post date. If the bill advances or the exclusion is indexed, the "stuck" framing needs a fresh angle.

Thirteen million American homeowners are frozen in place. If they sell their house, they could hand the IRS a tax bill they never planned for.

It isn't mortgage rates doing it. It's a tax law from 1997.

When you sell your primary home, you can shield $250,000 in gains if you're single, $500,000 if you're married. Congress set those limits in 1997 and never touched them. The National Association of Realtors estimates 13.1 million homeowners, about 15% of all owner-occupied households, are already sitting on gains above the line. Redfin puts a second number on the same trend. More than one in four homes, 25.9% of them, has gained at least $250,000 in value since it last sold.

So the owner runs the numbers, or the accountant does it for them, and the answer is stay put. They don't list, and they don't call an agent. The house quietly comes off the table.

Here's the part most agents miss. They're not waiting on rates to drop. They already decided. And while everyone keeps chasing the rate conversation, the homeowner who's frozen by taxes never hears from anybody.

So here's what you do now. Pull up your database, find one owner who's owned their home over ten years, and ask one question. Have you ever run the numbers on what selling would actually cost you in taxes? Most never have. And plenty owe far less than they think. That one question is how you get them talking again.

Thirteen million people think the door is closed. For a lot of them, it isn't.
