# A Judge Did Not Approve Private Listings.

> **WOW: Contrarian take against the exact conclusion the industry is forming this week. Every headline on Tuesday's ruling reads as a Compass and MRED win, and by Friday agents will be telling sellers a federal judge blessed private listings. He didn't. He held that Zillow hadn't made the strong showing required at the preliminary-injunction stage, on the relevant market, on MRED's market power, and on harm to competition rather than to Zillow itself. The question a seller actually cares about was never in front of him. This is the one script that keeps an agent from repeating a court case they don't understand in a listing appointment.**

Reported by Real Estate News 2026-09-15, The Real Deal Chicago 2026-09-15, HousingWire 2026-09-15, Inman 2026-09-16, Bloomberg 2026-09-16.

- **Claim:** "He decided an MLS can enforce its own rule."
  - Source: Memorandum Opinion, 2026-09-15, Dkt. 138, *Zillow Group, Inc. et al v. Midwest Real Estate Data LLC et al*, N.D. Ill. On the Sherman Section 2 exclusionary-conduct question the court wrote: "On the present record, Zillow has not sufficiently shown that MRED's threatened termination of the feed constitutes exclusionary maintenance of monopoly power rather than enforcement of its data distribution rules." The opinion's industry-background section characterizing MRED's October 2025 revision is headed "MRED Clarifies Its Listing Feed Rules," i.e. the court treated it as a clarification of a long-standing rule rather than a rule change aimed at Zillow. Quoted in Rob Hahn, "Fatality: On the Court's Decision in Zillow v. MRED," Notorious ROB, 2026-09-16 (premium), which embeds the opinion PDF.
  - Who was measured: n/a, legal holding.

- **Claim:** "He wrote that the record shows harm to Zillow more clearly than harm to competition."
  - Source: Opinion, 2026-09-15: "The record more clearly demonstrates harm to Zillow than harm to competition generally. The alleged agreement threatens Zillow with the loss of listing data and may make it more difficult for Zillow to implement its Standards or operate Zillow Preview as Zillow prefers. But disadvantage to a particular competitor does not, without more, establish reduced competition in the relevant market." Quoted verbatim in Hahn, Notorious ROB, 2026-09-16.
  - Who was measured: n/a, legal finding on antitrust injury.
  - Status: confirmed, quoted from the opinion. The spoken line is a close paraphrase that keeps the comparison intact. The exact sentence is available if D.J. prefers to quote it: "The record more clearly demonstrates harm to Zillow than harm to competition generally."

- **Supporting holdings, not spoken, available for caption depth:**
  - Relevant market: the court rejected Zillow's proposed "Residential Real Estate Listing Creation and Distribution" market outright. "MRED does not create listings; it operates an MLS that collects and distributes listing information among its members, including both Zillow and Compass. Compass is a brokerage whose agents market and sell real estate, that is, it creates listings. Zillow operates a platform that neither creates nor distributes listings but rather receives and displays them." The court concluded Zillow "has not made a strong showing that its proposed market... corresponds to the commercial realities of the industry or encompasses services that are reasonably interchangeable."
  - Section 1 agreement: the court found MRED and Compass acted independently and that Zillow had not established an agreement. On MRED's incentives: "MRED does not make any significant profit from listings being displayed on Zillow. MRED receives just $360 a year from Zillow as membership dues, and in exchange provides Zillow access to all of MRED's listing data, according to defendants' testimony."
  - Transparency: "the word 'transparency' is repeated over 300 times in the parties' briefs. But transparency is not, standing alone, a goal of the antitrust laws and opacity is not an anticompetitive effect under the Sherman Act."
  - All quoted from the opinion via Hahn, Notorious ROB, 2026-09-16.

### LinkedIn (PRIMARY)
**Caption:**
A federal judge did not approve private listings this week. By Friday, somebody is going to tell a seller he did.

Here is what Judge John Tharp actually decided on September 15.

He denied Zillow's motion for a preliminary injunction against MRED. He found Zillow "has not made the required strong showing that it is likely to succeed" on its antitrust claims. He found Zillow had not shown MRED "possesses monopoly power in a cognizable relevant market." He found Zillow had not made the showing necessary to establish antitrust injury, because it could not connect its own business losses to a reduction in competition in Chicagoland.

Then he dissolved the temporary restraining order that had been keeping Zillow's Chicago feed on, granted MRED's motion to compel arbitration, and stayed the claims against Compass while that arbitration runs.

Read that list again and notice what is not on it.

No holding on whether marketing a home privately before it hits the MLS is good or bad for the person selling the house. No holding on days to contract. No holding on sale price. That question was not in front of him, and no federal court has decided it. The earlier Compass suit against Zillow's Listing Access Standards ended in a voluntary dismissal in March, not a ruling.

MRED called it "a victory for fairness, transparency and broker cooperation." Compass said "choice belongs to the homeowner." Zillow said "this was not a final ruling" and "it was not a finding that MRED and Compass did nothing wrong."

All three of those statements can be true at once, because a preliminary-injunction denial is a statement about a burden of proof at an early stage. It is not a verdict on a business model.

Go look at what you plan to say to your next seller about going private or delayed. If a court case is carrying any weight in that pitch, take it out. Not because the case went the wrong way. Because the case did not answer the question your seller is asking.

One company did not prove its case. That is the whole ruling.
