# Eighteen thousand dollars, for what

> **WOW: Criterion 4 (a number the viewer computes alongside you) into criterion 6 (a named, checkable counterexample). Every agent knows their split as a percentage and almost none have converted it into a dollar figure with a year attached. The moment it becomes $18,000 it stops being a rate and becomes a purchase. Then two names they already respect prove the thing the $18,000 was supposedly buying is optional.**

> **Strongest Line:** "Neither one of them has a national brand behind them, and both of them are at the top of this city's leaderboard."

## Spoken Script (~68 seconds)

**THE SETUP**

If I were at one of the big firms handing over twenty percent of every check, here's what I'd be sitting there thinking.

**THE MATH**

Last year I sold twelve homes. Call it three hundred grand a piece. Commission's what, seventy-five hundred? Twenty percent of that is fifteen hundred dollars. On a deal I did ninety-nine percent of the work on. Times twelve. That's eighteen thousand dollars.

**THE LIST**

Did they give me eighteen thousand dollars worth of training? Because for eighteen grand I hire one of the best coaches in this business. I throw client appreciation parties. I sponsor events. I donate to a charity, I buy new furniture, whatever. And obviously the firm has to make money, I get that. But one deal a month is costing you eighteen thousand a year?

**THE OBJECTION**

And they'll tell you the brand matters. Really? There are fourteen hundred and eighty licensed brokerage companies with a Chicago address right now. You can name maybe twelve of them. So the other fourteen hundred and sixty-eight, those people must be crazy. Doing no deals at all.

**THE REVEAL**

Greg Pekarsky. Matt Laricy. Greg built his own firm. Matt runs his family's. Neither one of them has a national brand behind them, and both of them are at the top of this city's leaderboard.

**CLOSE**

So you're good giving away eighteen thousand a year for a logo. Maybe something to think about.

## Production Note

**This script needs the week's heat-4 slot.** Rule 9.2 caps heat 4-5 at one post across the
entire publishing schedule and the Wednesday take normally spends it. **Either the take lane
yields that week, or this does not run.** See the standard's note on the heat budget, which
this script is the reason for revisiting.

**Pattern interrupt (move 3, Counting).** The arithmetic is performed, not narrated. Count it
out on fingers, or on a phone calculator held where the lens can see it. The eighteen thousand
should appear on screen because D.J. just computed it, never as a graphic that was there
first. If the number is pre-made, the whole device dies.

**Perform the hesitation. Do not clean it up.** "Commission's what, seventy-five hundred?" is
the most important delivery note in the script. That upward inflection is what separates
somebody thinking from somebody reciting, and it is what makes the viewer run their own
numbers alongside instead of watching. **An editor or a rewrite will want to smooth that into
"commission is seventy-five hundred." Refuse it.** Same for "Call it three hundred grand" --
the looseness is the credibility.

**The concession is load-bearing and it is one clause.** "And obviously the firm has to make
money, I get that." Do not expand it, do not move it earlier, do not cut it. Without it the
video is a man angry at arithmetic. With it, he is reasonable and the number does the work,
which is the difference between a comment section and a pile-on (qualifying test 4).

**The list goes fast.** Coach, two client parties, sponsor a team, charity with your name on
it. Four items, no elaboration on any of them. The speed is the point -- it makes the money
feel like a foreclosed life rather than a line item. Slowing down to explain any one of them
turns it into a tip and drains the beat.

**The sarcasm is aimed at a claim, never at a person.** "They must be insane" is aimed at the
brand argument, and the loop-back immediately reveals it as irony. Delivery matters: dry, not
sneering. If it reads as contempt for anyone, it fails guardrail 1.

**No firm is named and none may be added.** "One of the big firms" stays a category. Ship
test: could a viewer name ONE company with confidence? Currently no, and the twelve-you-can-name
line must not turn into D.J. naming any of them.

**The two names are the payoff and they are the reason this version beats the first draft.**
The abstraction ("1,468 other firms") is a statistic; two people the viewer already respects is
proof. The sarcasm sets the trap -- "those people must be crazy, doing no deals at all" -- and
the names spring it one second later. **Deliver the names flat and unhurried, with a real beat
between them.** No build-up, no "and get this." The flatness is what makes it land as fact
rather than as a gotcha.

**Say what is true about them, which is better than what the rant said.** Neither man
self-sponsors. Greg Pekarsky co-founded Vesta Preferred; Matt Laricy is managing partner of his
family's firm, Americorp. They did not go without a brokerage -- **they decided a national brand
was not worth what it costs and built their own instead**, which is a sharper argument and one
that survives being checked. "Greg built his own firm, Matt runs his family's" is accurate,
respectful, and does more work than "they self sponsor" ever could.

**Both are named positively, and that is a different category from heat 5.** The banned move is
naming a party **as wrong**. These two are named as right, the way Sara DeWulf is named in
BP-001. The friction in this script points at the brand argument, never at a person or a firm.
**Nothing critical may be said about Vesta, Americorp, or either man** -- the instant the script
compares them to anyone, it becomes a competitive attack and the whole thing changes character.

**Courtesy call before it posts.** Greg has been a guest on Keeping It Real (2025-01-28) so the
relationship is warm; a heads-up is easy and probably welcome. **Matt has not been on the show,
and he should get a heads-up too.** Both will hear about this from someone regardless, and D.J.
runs a competing brokerage, so being told directly by him converts a possible irritation into a
likely reshare -- and, in Matt's case, plausibly into a guest booking. This is a two-minute text
and it is worth more than the video.

**Rung 2, and the close is the softest ask in the entire lane.** "Maybe something to think
about" is the whole CTA. It is not an instruction, it does not ask for a comment, and it leaves
the viewer holding the question rather than a task -- which is exactly what seeding doubt looks
like when it is done properly. **Do not strengthen it.** Every instinct in an edit will want to
end harder. Ending soft after that arithmetic is the move; the number already did the pushing.

**The "you can name maybe twelve" line stays unanswered.** D.J. never lists the twelve. Naming
even one turns a category into a target.

**First-hour ops.** Pin the IDFPR query link plus the two ranking citations as the first
comment, so the 1,480 and the leaderboard claim are both checkable in one click. That pinned
comment is the whole defense when somebody says he made it up. **Tag neither man in the post.**
Tagging turns a citation into a summons and puts them on the spot in a competitor's comment

**The honest limit, and it changes what may be claimed.**
Spot-checking the Chicago corporation names returns entries like property management firms,
commercial shops, and at least one industry data cooperative. **These 1,480 are not all
one-person residential sales brokerages, and the script must never say they are.** The script
as written says only that 1,480 licensed brokerage companies exist and that the viewer can name
about twelve, both of which are true. **Do not** upgrade it to "1,480 agents who work for
themselves," "1,480 solo agents," or anything implying they all chose independence over a
brand. That claim is false and the list is public, so somebody will check.

**What was cut and why.** D.J.'s original rant said "over 2,000 agents in Chicago who self
sponsor." IDFPR carries no self-sponsorship field, so the figure is not verifiable as stated.
The closest true numbers are 3,125 managing brokers (people licensed to sponsor themselves,
whether or not they do) and 1,480 brokerage companies. The 1,480 was chosen because it is the
one a viewer can confirm without interpretation. Rule 1, and the point survives the swap intact.

**The arithmetic (spoken).**
A worked example, explicitly hypothetical in the script ("call it three hundred grand"), not a
market claim:

- $300,000 sale price, 2.5% to the agent's side = $7,500 gross commission
- 20% brokerage split = $1,500 per closing
- 12 closings = **$18,000 per year**

Every figure is internally consistent. **The 2.5% is an assumption, not a published rate**, and
the script does not present it as one -- D.J. states the commission dollar figure directly and
lets the viewer substitute their own. Post-settlement, the agent's side is negotiated per deal,
so no average is available to cite and none should be invented.

**The conservative version is deliberate.** Chicago's actual median is well above $300,000 --
reporting through 2026 puts the city and Chicagoland range roughly $381,000 to $420,000
depending on source and geography. At $400,000 the same math yields $2,000 per closing and
**$24,000 a year.** **Two reasons the script keeps $300,000:** the lower figure cannot be
attacked as cherry-picked, and an agent whose real average is higher does that upgrade in their
own head, which is more persuasive than being told. If D.J. wants the bigger number, the median
must be sourced to a named report and date before air (Illinois REALTORS publishes monthly);
the $300,000 version needs no source at all because it is openly hypothetical.

**Pattern interrupt:** Confirmed, move 3 (Counting). Fingers or a visible calculator. The number arrives because he computed it.

**Why it should work:**
- **Hook mechanism (Heath):** the rehearsal frame is the whole engine. "If I were an agent, I'd walk in and say this" is not a claim to evaluate, it is a scene to watch, and watching somebody else's confrontation carries none of the defensiveness of being told about your own. By the time the viewer realizes the math is theirs, they have already agreed with it. It also converts a statistic into a *script the viewer can steal*, which is the most shareable object this brand makes (Family 9's mechanic arriving inside a Family 3 hook).
- **Share/save driver (Berger/Hormozi):** two independent drivers stacked. Practical value, because the math is portable to any split and any volume. And social currency, because forwarding it makes the sender look like someone who has run their own numbers. Expect saves and sends both, which is unusual -- most scripts get one or the other.
- **Retention move (MrBeast):** the arithmetic is a continuously resolving loop. Every clause pays off a number and opens the next one, so there is no drift point to defend against for the first forty seconds. The genuine risk is at "obviously the firm has to make money," which is a deliberate deceleration; the brand objection immediately after is what re-accelerates it.

**The dissent (your next A/B test):** two objections, and they are asymmetric.

The weak one is "you run a competing brokerage, of course you say this." The script survives it because D.J. concedes the firm's side out loud and names nobody, and because the arithmetic is checkable regardless of who says it.

The real one is the twenty percent itself. **A commenter will say the split buys errors-and-omissions coverage, legal and compliance review, a managing broker on the hook for their license, transaction coordination, and the office.** That is a genuine list and the script's "did you give me eighteen thousand worth of training" narrows the comparison to training alone, which is the script's softest joint. **Two ways to answer, and they are a real A/B.** Variant A ships as written and lets the objection arrive, because an agent defending their brokerage's value in the replies is doing the lane's work for it -- they are now itemizing what they get, which is precisely the audit the lane wants. Variant B widens the line to "eighteen thousand dollars worth of anything," which is harder to counter but also less specific and therefore less quotable. **Recommendation: run A, and have the reply ready.** The reply is not a rebuttal, it is "good, now write that list down and see if it comes to eighteen thousand" -- which is rung 2 arriving in the comment section.

**The second dissent, specific to the names:** somebody will point out that Vesta and Americorp *are* brands, and that both men are principals of their own firms rather than agents with no brokerage. **That is correct**, which is exactly why the script says "Greg built his own firm, Matt runs his family's" instead of the original "they self sponsor." The claim as written is only that neither has a *national* brand behind him, which is true and is the claim the $18,000 is about. **If anyone in the replies pushes on it, agree immediately** -- "right, they built their own, that's the point" is a stronger position than the one being attacked, and conceding it fast makes D.J. look like he knows the market rather than like he got caught.

**Recruiting test (guardrail 3):** a good agent watches a brokerage president do arithmetic unflattering to brokerages, concede the brokerage's side without being asked, name no competitor as wrong, praise two competitors' principals by name, and leave without asking for anything. Respect goes up on every one of those. **The two things that would flip it:** naming a firm as bad, which turns arithmetic into an attack and makes the concession read as a setup; and mentioning Kale, which retroactively converts the two names into props in an ad. **Neither happens in this script and neither may be added.**
