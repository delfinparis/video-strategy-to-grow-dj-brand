# A name clients can't say

> **WOW: The entire brand premium rests on one assumption nobody in this business has ever asked a client to confirm. This script does not argue the assumption is wrong. It hands the agent a three-text test that settles it tonight, and then prices whichever answer comes back.**

> **Strongest Line:** "If they answer with your name instead, you're the brand."

## Spoken Script (30-35 seconds, 81 words)

**HOOK (0:00-0:01.5)**
You're paying for a name clients can't say.

**TENSION (0:01.5-0:06.5)**
The extra you pay only works if the client cares. You've never asked.

**THE POINT (0:06.5-0:21.5)**
Text three past clients. Ask them to name your brokerage. No hints. If they answer with your name instead, you're the brand. If they can't answer at all, you paid every closing for something that never reached them.

**PAYOFF (0:21.5-0:30)**
Either way, find out what another firm here charges for it. Then look at what the name they can't say costs you.

*Word count: 81 spoken words (target 68-84, hard cap 88). Beat check: hook 8, tension 13, point 38, payoff 22. If the take runs long, cut "No hints" and let the instruction carry it. Do not cut "If they answer with your name instead, you're the brand," and do not cut the last line, which is the loop-back.*

## Production Note

- **Family 4, not the brief's first suggestion, and not Family 1.** The planner offered 4, 7 or 6. Family 6 is out on principle: it requires a sourced number and this entry has none that may be spoken. Family 7 was available but the lane has opened three of its last six scripts on the word "Nobody" (BP-009, BP-012, and the pattern is becoming a tic), so the cost goes first instead. The bank's own heat note for F5 asks for the wrong default named in the hook to clear heat 4, and "you're paying for a name clients can't say" does it in eight words.
- **Pattern interrupt: The Whip.** Hold the camera on a for-sale sign rider, a window decal, any hanging brokerage name for about a second, then whip to face on the word "name." The sign is the thing being priced, and it is the one prop in this lane that costs nothing to find. BP-014 ran The Stop and BP-013 ran The Push-In, so the move is clean on the two-post rule. Do not linger on the sign long enough for anyone to read which company it is. If the only sign on the walk is identifiable, shoot the whip off a blurred one or off a row of them.
- **Nothing in this script is a claim about clients, and that is deliberate.** The temptation here is enormous: the consumer research in the Data Source block says buyers do not rate the national brand as important, and saying so would feel like the stronger script. It is the weaker one. A stat about buyers in general is arguable and lets the viewer file it under "not my clients." A text message to three of their own past clients is not arguable. The receipt the viewer generates beats the receipt D.J. could cite, which is the only reason this entry ships with no source on camera.
- **Heat 4.5, and where the 4.5 lives.** Not in the target, which stays the big-brand premium as a category. It lives in "you paid every closing for something that never reached them," which is a flat verdict on the arrangement. No firm, franchise, or product is named. Ship test: could a viewer name ONE company with confidence? No. The sign in the whip has to stay unreadable or the ship test fails on the footage rather than the words.
- **Both outcomes of the test are good for the viewer and neither is good for the premium.** That is the structural trick and it should be delivered flat, not smugly. If the clients name the agent, the agent is the brand and the premium bought nothing. If the clients cannot answer, the premium bought a name that never arrived. D.J. does not point this out on camera. The viewer gets there on the walk to their phone.
- **Rung 2, with a rung-1 test inside the payload.** The test is the argument, not the ask, and the ask is the market look in the payoff. Miller will call this two CTAs (see the council block); it is one motion, and the order matters. Test first because it costs nothing and produces the number-shaped feeling. Market look second because that is the rung that makes an agent explore.
- **Kale is not mentioned and cannot be.** "Another firm here" is the whole permitted universe. Chicago is implied by "here" and by the walk.
- **Recruiting test, read twice:** a good agent watches a brokerage president tell them to go text their own clients rather than trust his research, and then send them to a competitor's fee schedule. Respect goes up. The one delivery that would flip it is any hint of triumph on the second outcome, which turns a test into a taunt.
- **First-hour ops:** no link to pin. The pinned comment is the part that did not fit and the one hedge the script owes. **Comment duty applies** and this one will produce the lane's best replies: agents reporting what their clients actually said. Flag every Chicago one into Close.

Three texts is three texts. It is not research and I would not run a listing presentation off it. What it is good for is ending an assumption you have been paying for without ever checking, and three is enough to do that.

Pick clients from at least a year back, because recent ones are still looking at your paperwork. And ask it plainly: what is the name of the company I work for. If you soften it into "do you remember where I work," you will get a polite yes that tells you nothing.

The answer people get most often is their own name, and that is the interesting one.

**Why it should work:**
- **Hook mechanism (Heath):** this is a clean gap, not a conclusion. "A name clients can't say" opens a question the viewer cannot answer about their own business, which is the Aha-after-Huh structure, and the script closes the loop by handing over the instrument rather than the answer. Curse of Knowledge risk is low because there is no jargon: a text message and a name. Heath's caution: "the extra you pay" has to be unmistakably the brand premium, so it must follow the hook immediately and not be separated by a breath.
- **Share/save driver (Berger):** high-arousal anger, pointed at the arrangement, with the viewer as the wronged party and possibly as the hero (variant 3 makes that explicit). Social Currency is strong because the retell is a dare an agent can hand a friend. One-sentence retell: "text three past clients and see if they can name your brokerage." D.J.'s point survives compression completely, which is the test.
- **Retention move (MrBeast):** the instruction lands at second seven and the two outcomes are a branching payoff, which is the strongest retention shape this lane has produced. Nothing to wait for and nothing dull. His note: the second outcome must not be longer than the first, because a viewer who has already decided which one they are stops listening.

**The dissent (your next A/B test):** **Miller objects to the close.** The script asks for a text message and then asks for a market comparison, which by his rule is two CTAs, which is zero. He wants the payoff cut to the test alone and the market look saved for the pinned comment. **Hormozi opposes, hard:** the test produces a feeling and the market look produces a number, and a script that ends on a feeling is the weakest post of the week by his measure. **Kane sides with Hormozi** on the narrower ground that the lane has to keep rung 2 in the spoken script or the rung-2 share in the frontmatter becomes a fiction. **Decision: keep both, ship as written.** The two are one motion rather than two asks, they run in the order of increasing cost, and the lane's own standard says rung 2 is the engine and the beat that gets skipped. **The A/B:** this close against a test-only close on the next F-flavor script, judged on comment mix. The signal to watch is not comment volume, it is the ratio of "here is what my clients said" replies to "now I want to know what mine costs" replies. The first is recognition. The second is doubt, and doubt is what the lane is for.
