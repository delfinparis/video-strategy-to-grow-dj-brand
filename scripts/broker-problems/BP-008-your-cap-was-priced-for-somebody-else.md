# Your cap was priced for somebody else

> **WOW: The cap is the headline benefit of every split pitch and most agents never reach it, so they pay into a structure whose best feature is priced for the top of the office. The arithmetic is performed on camera on a hypothetical the viewer replaces with their own, and the close sends them to check three other firms' caps, one they would never join included, which is exploring dressed as an audit.**

> **Strongest Line:** "You'd have to double your business before it ever protects you."

## Spoken Script (30-35 seconds, 83 words)

**HOOK (0:00-0:01.5)**
Your cap was priced for somebody else.

**TENSION (0:01.5-0:06.5)**
The headline of the pitch, and you've never once reached it.

**THE POINT (0:06.5-0:21.5)**
Run it with me. Your split, times your average commission, times last year's deals. Say twenty percent, seventy-five hundred, ten deals. Fifteen thousand. The cap? Twenty-five? Thirty? You'd have to double your business before it ever protects you.

**PAYOFF (0:21.5-0:30)**
So find out what three other firms cap at, one you'd never join included, and where your number lands. Then you'll know who it was priced for.

*Word count: 83 spoken words (target 68-84, hard cap 88). Beat check: hook 7, tension 11, point 38, payoff 27. If the take runs long, cut "one you'd never join included." Do not cut the hesitations ("Twenty-five? Thirty?") and do not clean up "say twenty percent."*

## Production Note

- **Pattern interrupt: One Prop.** Phone calculator up on "run it with me," held where the lens can see it. The number appears because D.J. just computed it. If it is pre-made the device dies. Tuesday used The Stop and Thursday used Walk-Toward, so the move rotates.
- **Perform the arithmetic, do not present it.** This is the Run The Math With Me discipline borrowed into a Slow Leak. "Say twenty percent" and "Twenty-five? Thirty?" are the technique. The upward inflection is what makes a viewer run their own numbers alongside instead of watching. An editor or a rewrite will want to smooth these into clean statements. Refuse.
- **The cap figures are questions, not claims.** "Twenty-five? Thirty?" is D.J. guessing out loud at the viewer's cap so the viewer substitutes theirs. Caps vary widely by firm and market and no average is cited because none should be. Never turn the question into "most caps are around."
- **The split and commission are a worked hypothetical.** Same conservative model as BP-005: $300,000 sale, 2.5% side, $7,500, 20% split, ten deals, $15,000. Chicago's real median is higher and the figure is deliberately low so nobody can call it cherry-picked; an agent with a bigger average does the upgrade in their own head.
- **The hook and the close are the same sentence with the answer withheld.** "Priced for somebody else" opens; "then you'll know who it was priced for" closes. The viewer supplies the answer, which is the top producer in their office, and D.J. never says it.
- **"One you'd never join included" is doing lane work.** It signals the ask is research, not shopping, which is what keeps rung 2 from reading as a pitch. It also makes the ask feel safe, since checking a firm you would never join costs nothing socially. Keep it unless the take runs over 35.
- **The agent with no cap is not the target and the script does not pretend otherwise.** Traditional split firms with no cap exist. Those agents will say "I don't have a cap" in the replies, and the reply is "then you're paying the split with no ceiling at all, run the same math." See the council dissent for the A/B that broadens the hook.
- **No concession clause, and that is a judgment call.** Run The Math With Me carries "obviously the firm has to make money." This script is 83 words and does not. The friction is aimed at the pricing of a benefit, not at the firm making money, so it stays inside qualifying test 4 without it. If the read comes out hot, add "and firms need a margin, fine" after "fifteen thousand" and cut "one you'd never join included" to pay for it.
- **No firm is named and none may be added.** "The pitch" is every split pitch. Ship test: can a viewer name ONE company with confidence? No.
- **Kale is not mentioned** anywhere, ever, in this lane. Kale's own cap exists and it is exactly the thing this script may never bring up. Be right in public.
- **Recruiting test:** a brokerage president says the cap is priced for the top of the office and tells agents to go compare three firms' caps. Respect goes up. A named firm or a Kale mention would flip it, and neither happens.
- **Comment duty applies.** The replies to flag: agents posting their own split-times-deals number, and "I've never hit mine." Both are rung 1 arriving free. Flag every Chicago one into Close.

## Data Source

**The arithmetic (spoken).** A worked example, explicitly hypothetical ("say twenty percent"), not a market claim:

- $300,000 sale price, 2.5% to the agent's side = $7,500 gross commission (the 2.5% is an assumption; post-settlement there is no citable average and none is invented)
- 20% brokerage split = $1,500 per closing
- 10 closings = **$15,000 per year** paid toward a cap

**The cap figures (spoken as questions).** "Twenty-five? Thirty?" are guesses D.J. voices so the viewer substitutes their own. No firm's cap is cited and no average exists to cite. The script must never state a typical cap as fact.

**"You'd have to double your business."** Follows from the hypothetical: $15,000 paid against a $25,000 to $30,000 cap requires roughly seventeen to twenty deals at the same numbers. Rhetorical, and internally consistent with the example; not a claim about any agent's actual production.

**Supporting context, not spoken.** Consumer Federation of America, 2024: 49% of agents sold one or zero homes that year. Logged in `docs/recruiting-objections.md`. It is why the cap is a top-decile feature, and it stays off camera because the power of this script is the viewer computing their own number, not being told a statistic (bank entry A2's own rule).

**What is not claimed.** No typical cap, no share of agents who hit their cap, no dollar figure for any firm. Nothing here needs a source because everything spoken is either hypothetical or the viewer's own.

**Who-profits support:** category-level. No brokerage, franchise, coach, or product is named or identifiable.

**Do-not-cite check:** clean.

## Council Review

**Convened:** Hormozi, MrBeast, Kane, Welsh, Miller. **Witnesses:** Heath, Berger.

**Scroll-stop variants (spoken, pick one to A/B):**
1. "Your cap was priced for somebody else."  [hook_family: 4 System Indictment | emotion: loss] The default. Reassigns the headline benefit in seven words and withholds who.
2. "The best thing in your split was priced for somebody else."  [hook_family: 4 System Indictment | emotion: loss] Kane and Welsh's pick. Broadens the hook to every agent on a split, cap or no cap, at the cost of four words and some sharpness.
3. "Fifteen thousand dollars a year toward a number you'll never reach."  [hook_family: 6 Named Stakes | emotion: alarm] Hormozi's pick. Fastest to the money. Heath's objection: the number is a hypothetical, and opening on it as if it were the viewer's own is the exact move the Data Source says not to make.

**Pattern interrupt:** Confirmed, One Prop. The calculator has to be visible and the number has to appear because D.J. just computed it.

**Why it should work:**
- **Hook mechanism (Heath):** the hook takes a thing the viewer was sold as *for them* and says it was for somebody else, then withholds who. The loop stays open until the last line and the viewer closes it themselves, which is the Aha-after-Huh shape. The Curse of Knowledge risk is "cap." Agents on cap firms know it instantly. Agents on no-cap splits do not, and variant 2 exists for them.
- **Share/save driver (Berger/Hormozi):** practical value, because the arithmetic is portable to any split, and Social Currency, because forwarding it says "I ran mine." Expect saves and sends both, the same profile as BP-005. Retell test: "the cap is priced for the top producers, not for you." Survives.
- **Retention move (MrBeast):** the arithmetic is a continuously resolving loop, every clause pays off a number and opens the next, and there is no drift point until the ask. The hesitations are decelerations by design; they are also what makes the viewer compute alongside, so they stay.

**The dissent (your next A/B test):** **Kane and Welsh hold that the hook excludes half the audience.** Plenty of agents are on a straight split with no cap, and "your cap" tells them in the first two words this video is not for them. **Hormozi and Miller hold the sharp version:** "your cap" is a specific object and specificity is what stops a scroll; the no-cap agent still stays because "priced for somebody else" is a mystery regardless. **Decision: ship variant 1, because the lane's mechanic is recognition, and "your cap" produces "that's mine" faster than any broader line.** The A/B: variant 1 against variant 2, judged on 3-second retention and on comment mix. If variant 2 draws the same volume of agents posting their own numbers with fewer "I don't have a cap" replies, Kane wins and the lane learns that broader beats sharper on arithmetic scripts.

**Welsh's note, not a test:** "Run it with me" is now a recurring device across BP-005 and this script. It is the lane's most series-able move. Use it once every two or three weeks and vary what gets computed: the split (done), the cap (this), a monthly fee structure (E1 angle 2, open).

**Recruiting test (10.3), read twice:** a brokerage president tells agents the cap is priced for the top of the office and sends them to compare three firms' caps, including one they would never join. More respect. The one thing that would flip it is bringing up any specific firm's cap, and that includes his own. Neither happens.
