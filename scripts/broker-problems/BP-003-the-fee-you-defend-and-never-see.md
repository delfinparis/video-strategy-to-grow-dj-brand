# The fee you defend and never see

> **WOW: Criterion 4 (a surprising number) into criterion 2 (contrarian reframe). Every agent has read this fee as a wallet problem, which is why nobody reacts to it -- it isn't their wallet. Reframing it as a credibility problem puts it somewhere they actually care about, which is the closing table with a client looking at them.**

> **Strongest Line:** "You're going to get asked about that line again this year. Right now the honest answer is you don't know either."

## Spoken Script (~60 seconds)

**THE THING**

Your brokerage charges your client a fee at closing, and you're the one who has to explain it.

**THE SIZE**

There's a report out last month from the Consumer Policy Center, and I want to be careful with it, because the authors say straight out it's built mostly on anecdotal evidence. Agent interviews, social posts, trade reporting. Not a clean dataset. What they found is these administrative fees usually run four to six hundred dollars, and at some firms north of two thousand. So run it against your own year. Twelve closings, five hundred a piece, that's six thousand dollars your clients paid. You saw none of it. And you're the one sitting at the table when somebody points at that line and asks what it's for.

**THE MIRROR**

So find out two things this week. What the number is on your side, and who at your brokerage can actually tell you what it pays for. Ask in writing.

**LOOP-BACK**

You're going to get asked about that line again this year. Right now the honest answer is you don't know either.

## Production Note

**Pattern interrupt (move 5, One Prop).** A closing statement in hand, held up on the hook with a fee line visible. Do not zoom, do not narrate it, do not show a real client's document -- use a redacted or dummy statement. Drop it on "explain it," bring it back on "points at that line."

**Re-hook placement.** "Six thousand dollars your clients paid. You saw none of it." at roughly 0:33. That is where a fee video usually dies, and the reversal is what keeps it alive -- the viewer has spent thirty seconds assuming this is about their money and it turns out to be about their reputation. Land "You saw none of it" flat, then a beat.

**The arithmetic is a worked example, not a claim.** Twelve closings at five hundred is D.J. showing the math out loud with round numbers, and it has to sound like that. Do not say "the average agent pays." Say "run it against your own year." The viewer substitutes their own numbers, which is the point, and it also keeps the script clear of a statistic nobody has.

**Heat 3.5, and check the budget first.** Confirm the take lane has not surrendered its heat-4 slot, and confirm this week's Wednesday take is still holding it. This script sits at 3.5 as written. It would slide to 4 if the close moved from "ask in writing" to anything implying the brokerage is hiding it, so do not let the delivery do what the words do not.

**No name, in any direction.** No brokerage, no franchise, no closing-platform, and the report itself does not name firms either. "Some firms" stays a category.

**Beat 3 discipline.** "Ask in writing" is the whole action and it is deliberately small. It is not a confrontation, it is a question anyone is entitled to ask, and an agent who asks it will find out something either way -- including from how long the answer takes.

**First-hour ops.** Pin the report link with the sample caveat in the pinned text. **Comment duty applies:** this script will pull agents naming their own fee amounts in the replies, which is the single best comment outcome this lane produces. Flag Chicago names into Close.

## Data Source

**The report (spoken).**
Consumer Policy Center, Stephen Brobeck (Senior Fellow) and Wendy Gilch (Fellow), *"'Junk' Fees Charged to Both Home Sellers and Buyers,"* published **2026-07-28**.
Coverage: https://www.realestatenews.com/2026/07/28/junk-brokerage-fees-cost-consumers-2b-annually-report-says

- **Spoken figures:** administrative fees typically **$400 to $600**; full range **$200 to more than $2,000**.
- **Not spoken, on purpose:** the report's roughly **$2 billion** annual aggregate consumer-cost estimate, and its agent-sourced estimate that **30% of listing agents and 60-75% of buyer agents** charge a fee. Both are the most quotable numbers in the report and both are the least defensible, because they are estimates built on the anecdotal base the authors disclose. A big round number in a hook is exactly the Rule 1 failure mode. Keep them out of the script; they can go in the pinned comment with the caveat attached.
- **Status:** verified against coverage of the report 2026-08-18. **Re-verify before air**, and if the primary CPC document is reachable by then, cite that directly rather than the trade coverage.

**The caveat is spoken and does not get cut.**
The authors state the work is "based largely on anecdotal evidence" owing to a "paucity of collective data" on brokerage fee practices. The script says so in the second sentence. That is Rule 1 and it is also the reason the video survives its own comment section -- somebody in the replies will point out the report is thin, and the video already said so, which converts a critic into a corroborator.

**The organization name.** This is the **Consumer Policy Center**, not the Consumer Federation of America. The two get conflated in secondary coverage and Brobeck has been associated with both. Say CPC or say "a consumer research group" -- do not say CFA.

**The arithmetic.** Twelve closings at $500 is a worked example using the midpoint of the reported typical range against a round transaction count. It is not a claim about any agent's actual volume or fee, and the script frames it as "run it against your own year" for that reason. Do not convert it into an average.

**The reversal claim is not a statistic.** "You saw none of it" describes the ordinary structure of a brokerage-charged administrative fee. If a viewer's firm splits that fee with agents, they will say so in the replies, and that is a fine outcome -- it is a real variation, not a correction, and the honest response in the thread is "then you're in the better version of this."

**Do-not-cite check:** clean.

**Scroll-stop variants (spoken, pick one to A/B):**

**Pattern interrupt:** Confirmed, move 5 (One Prop). Redacted closing statement, fee line visible, up on the hook and back on "points at that line." Never a real client's document.

**Why it should work:**
- **Hook mechanism (Heath):** it is concrete and it is a scene, not an abstraction. "Sitting at the table when somebody points at that line" is a moment nearly every producing agent has actually lived, and it retrieves a specific memory rather than proposing an idea. The gap it opens is not "is this true" but "what did I say that time," which is a harder gap to leave open.
- **Share/save driver (Berger/Hormozi):** practical value plus mild outrage, which is the combination that travels. The share here is likely to be public rather than private, unlike BP-002, because the agent forwarding it is not admitting anything about their own standing. Expect Facebook to carry this one -- broker-forward behavior in the Rule 9.5 map.
- **Retention move (MrBeast):** the reversal at 0:33 is the retention device. The video appears to be about money for its whole first half and turns out to be about the agent's credibility, which resets the viewer's model of what they are watching at exactly the drift point.

**The dissent (your next A/B test):** the strongest objection is structural, not factual. "This is a consumer-protection story, not an agent story, and D.J. is borrowing consumer outrage for a recruiting lane." That objection has teeth, and the script's answer is the reversal beat -- the agent is the one holding the bag at the table, which makes it genuinely their problem and not a borrowed one. If the reversal is delivered weakly, the objection is correct and the video is a repurposed news story. **Watch this in the edit.** The A/B: Variant A as written; Variant B opens on the closing-table scene ("You've had a client point at a line on the settlement statement and ask you what it is") and holds the report until beat 2, making the agent's experience the entry point rather than the news. Variant B is likely stronger and is only second here because it is the riskier rewrite. Judge on completion rate and on whether repliers post their own fee amounts.

**Recruiting test (guardrail 3):** a good agent watches a brokerage president raise a fee practice that brokerages benefit from, name no company, ask for nothing, and hand over a question they can ask their own broker on Monday. That is the recruiting mechanism, and it works only because nothing is being sold. The instant a Kale line appears anywhere in this script, the whole thing reads as a competitor ad and the comment section goes quiet.
